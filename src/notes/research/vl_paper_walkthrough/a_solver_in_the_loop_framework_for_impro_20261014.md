# A Solver-in-the-Loop Framework for Improving LLMs on Answer Set Programming for Logic Puzzle Solving
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `schrader2025solver`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17093)

## scope and result

The paper trains language models to emit Answer Set Programming (ASP) encodings for grid logic puzzles, using an ASP solver both to label training continuations and to rank alternatives at inference. Its task instance is (I={D,E,H,S}): natural-language description, entities and types, hints, and the unique solution. The model emits a choice-rule encoding for the entities and then one partial encoding for each hint; the solver checks whether the ground-truth solution remains among the accumulated program’s answer sets. This study covers two components: automatic solver-labeled instruction-tuning data and solver-guided best-of-N generation with regeneration and backtracking.

Inference: the transferable idea for VeruLaw is to make a verifier's counterexample or proof result a training label and a search score, rather than asking an LLM to trust its own code. The paper studies ASP, not Rust or Verus, so the transfer below is an engineering analogy.

## technique: solver-labelled trajectory data

What it is. A trajectory alternates prompts, natural-language hints, and generated ASP fragments. The authors state: “We define a trajectory T to be an alternating sequence of natural language inputs and ASP statements.” (Sampling Trajectories, p. 3.) It starts with a prompt containing general ASP instructions, (D), and (E). The reference model (M_S) emits ΓC,E, the entity and choice-rule encoding. For each hint (h_i), it emits Γi, yielding (T={P_{D,E},Γ_{C,E},h_1,Γ_1,…,h_n,Γ_n}).

How to apply it. (1) Build a prompt with the problem description, entity declarations, and general ASP instructions, including a basic choice rule (Sampling Trajectories and footnote 1, p. 3). (2) Append hints one at a time and sample five completions at each step; the paper used temperature 0.8 for this data collection (Classification of ASP Encodings, p. 3). (3) Concatenate the base and the first (k) fragments. (4) Run the ASP solver and check whether the ground-truth solution is among its answer sets. (5) Label a fragment chosen when the partial program includes the ground-truth answer without solver errors or warnings; label it rejected when it gives only wrong answer sets, is unsatisfiable, or produces solver errors or warnings (Classification of ASP Encodings, p. 3). (6) Generate trajectories by depth-first search, retaining at most two chosen continuations per step; form chosen/rejected preference pairs by their Cartesian product, and retain mixed cases so the data emphasizes decisions where the solver distinguishes alternatives. If all five responses are chosen or all are rejected, continue without forming preference pairs. When all are rejected, omit that hint and its rejected response before proceeding to the next hint (Training Data Generation, p. 4). (7) Fine-tune with supervised causal language modelling; the authors say, “The chosen instances can be used to train LLMs using SFT with causal language modeling.” (Model Training, p. 4.)

Verus form. Inference: represent each generated Rust/proof fragment as a candidate continuation of a specification or proof function. Compile and run Verus on the accumulated module; classify a continuation as chosen when the verifier discharges the target postcondition and rejected when it reports an error. Keep the base Rust implementation and add one lemma, invariant, or `ensures` clause at a time. Use proof functions and `assert`/`assert forall` facts as the generated fragments; executable functions retain ordinary Rust bodies. Unlike ASP's answer-set comparison, Verus needs a complete verification obligation and may require explicit loop invariants. This mapping is an inference, not a claim made by the paper.

Evidence and limits. The data-generation setup uses LogicPuzzles' 50 training instances and samples (N=5) alternatives. Llama-3.3 70B produced 3.7 chosen and 1.3 rejected responses per input on average (standard deviation 1.82); Qwen3 32B produced 3.3 and 1.7 (standard deviation 2.04) (Training Data Statistics, p. 5). The authors retained 1,546 Llama and 1,060 Qwen training instances. They acknowledge, “Yet, this also implies that even the chosen instances might not be perfect.” (Discussion, p. 7.) Inference: preserving the ground-truth answer does not establish that a fragment faithfully translates its hint or excludes every incorrect answer. Inference: a Verus label similarly proves only the supplied specification and trusted assumptions, so specification quality and external-body contracts remain outside this feedback signal.

## technique: solver-grounded greedy best-of-N with recovery

What it is. At test time, the model proposes alternatives for the base encoding and then for each hint. A reward ranks partial programs. The paper defines (f_r(Γ,M)=1/M-1_E(Γ)-1_U(Γ)-1_{NE}(Γ)), where (M) is the number of answer sets, (1_E) flags errors or warnings, (1_U) unsatisfiability, and (1_{NE}) exceeding a configured answer-set maximum (Reward Function, p. 4). The authors write, “Our test-time method is based on greedy search”; it maximizes the current reward without trading it for future rewards (Sampling Procedure, p. 4).

How to apply it. (1) Generate (N) alternatives for the base choice rule. (2) Score each with the solver, replacing the reciprocal term 1/M with a strict check for (n!)^(m−1) answer sets for an unconstrained m×n puzzle. (3) Keep the highest-scoring base. (4) For each hint in order, generate (N) alternatives appended to the selected prefix and keep the highest reward. (5) When all new alternatives have negative rewards, use the recovery mechanisms: regeneration produces (2N) additional alternatives; backtracking returns to the previous hint with maximum reward that had more than one alternative, then regenerates all later hints (Sampling Procedure, p. 4). The reported inference settings use (N=5), temperature 1.0, and at most five backtracking steps for LogicPuzzles and one for GridPuzzles (Models, p. 5).

Verus form. Inference: generate (N) candidate proof continuations, run Verus on each, and rank verified candidates above failed ones; a finer score can prefer fewer diagnostics or lower verification time. Use a state record containing the current Rust module, proof prefix, and target. Regeneration makes fresh candidates after all fail. Backtracking restores the latest prefix with multiple candidates. Verus has no paper-defined numeric reward or answer-set count, so these scoring details are an inference. A candidate must use real Verus constructs (`spec fn`, `proof fn`, `requires`, `ensures`, `invariant`, `assert`) and should never call proof code at run time.

Evidence. Table 2 reports Llama-3.3 70B on LogicPuzzles: SFT alone gives 55.4% accuracy; sequential best-of-5 gives 62.7%; adding regeneration gives 64.6%; adding backtracking gives 62.7%; both recovery mechanisms give 66.8%; and best-of-25 gives 69.2%. The metric requires a unique correct final answer, with a Levenshtein fallback for representation differences (Evaluation Metrics and Datasets, p. 4; Table 2, p. 6). The paper’s test-set counts conflict: p. 4 describes “124 grid-based puzzles (50 train and 74 test)” and footnote 2 says it removes 26 overlapping instances, whereas the appendix describes the full evaluation as “all 100 dataset instances” (Evaluation on the Full LogicPuzzles Test Split, p. 14). Table 3 distinguishes cleaned and full results (p. 15). The paper does not reconcile these counts, so this study does not assign Table 2 an independently verified denominator. GPT-4.1-mini rises from 39.2% to 55.4% with sequential best-of-5, a +16.2 percentage-point change (Table 2, p. 6). Inference: these results support verifier-guided selection on this benchmark, but do not establish that larger N is always cost-effective; the authors state, “Furthermore, the number of generated output tokens scales linearly with N.” They call N=5 a trade-off for their setup and exclude input tokens from the cost proxy (Cost Comparisons and footnote 4, p. 6).

## technique: staged prompt pipeline and solver boundary

The comparison pipeline emits six stages: structured constants, ASP formatting, predicates, the choice rule, rewriting complex logical OR constraints, and final translation of all natural-language constraints (Settings, p. 5). Only the sixth stage yields a complete encoding, so intermediate solver analysis is unavailable. Inference: a Verus agent can expose proof checkpoints after each specification or invariant addition, preserving the paper's staged decomposition while making each checkpoint verifiable. The boundary matters: the paper's solver can reject a partial encoding early, whereas a Verus obligation may remain underconstrained until a function's complete contract is present.

## what is proved, trusted, and left open

The experiments execute generated ASP with clingo 5.7.1 (Models, p. 5). During training, chosen fragments preserve the ground-truth answer; during inference, the reward checks solver diagnostics and answer-set counts without access to ground truth (Classification of ASP Encodings, p. 3; Reward Function, p. 4). Inference: a high inference reward cannot establish semantic fidelity to the natural-language puzzle. LogicPuzzles uses 3×4 puzzles; GridPuzzles includes 3×4, 3×5, 4×4, 4×5, and 4×6 puzzles and is used only for evaluation (Evaluation Metrics and Datasets, p. 4). The appendix reports that some of the largest puzzles cannot be solved within computational constraints (Results on GridPuzzles by Size and Difficulty, pp. 14–15). Inference: the solver, dataset labels, evaluation heuristic, prompt, and reward design determine what this feedback establishes. The authors explicitly note, “One important strength of our method is that it requires no additional human annotations as it samples ASP statements from LLMs and automatically computes a reward or categorizes them into chosen and rejected.” (Discussion, p. 7.) They also caution that automatically chosen instances may be imperfect and propose weighted rewards and reinforcement learning as future work (Discussion and Outlook, p. 7).

Inference: for agent-written verified Rust, a solver loop can reduce syntactic and local proof failures, but it cannot detect a wrong functional specification, an omitted safety property, unsound assumptions about external code, or an unsafe use of `external_body`. The paper's evidence is on puzzle accuracy, not on Rust safety or Verus soundness. Inference: VL should build on the basic generate–solver-label–fine-tune pattern; the paper already supplies that pattern and its recovery ablation. A useful open task is measuring whether verifier-guided proof search remains stable across dependency and specification changes.

## techniques for the catalog

- solver-labelled trajectory data: generate partial formal programs, run a solver after each step, and fine-tune on chosen continuations; rejected continuations identify mixed cases during data collection
- greedy best-of-N verifier search: sample alternatives, rank partial programs by verifier feedback, and recover with regeneration and backtracking
- staged ASP formalization (comparison pipeline): generate constants, format them, generate predicates and a choice rule, rewrite natural-language constraints, then translate them into ASP
