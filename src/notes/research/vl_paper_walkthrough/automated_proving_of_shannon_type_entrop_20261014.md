## problem, evidence, and trust

The paper trains 0.6B and 1.7B Qwen3 models to emit one atomic entropy proof step at a time, either a Shannon-type information measure or a positive multiple of a supplied condition, then searches over proof trees. The symbolic checker accepts a proof when repeated residual subtraction reaches zero. The authors state: “The proof succeeds if we reach Rt = 0.” (Section II-B, PDF p. 2.) On a held-out final set of 60 inequalities with 10–15 random variables, the 0.6B model proves 51/60 (85.0%) with the selected tree search; GPT-5.5 zero-shot proves 1/60 (1.7%), Claude-4.6 Sonnet proves none, and Psitip proves 20/60 (33.3%) (Table IV and Section IV-F, PDF p. 5). The denominators are the 60 final-test inequalities, while the 30-item easy and hard sets used for ablations have separate denominators. All page references below count PDF pages.

Inference: proof acceptance depends on the symbolic checker, entropy definitions, and supplied conditions; beam scoring and pruning affect which proofs are found. Inference: a passing decomposition proves the encoded inequality under its conditions, not that it expresses the intended property. The paper's LP baselines use a 300-second limit (Section IV-E, p. 4), so their comparison is time-bounded rather than a completeness claim. Table IV reports Psitip solving all mixed-condition samples at n=10–11 and none at n=12–15; Section IV-E instead says “PSITIP can solve all the n ≤ 12 samples but time out in bigger sample.” This study follows the table. For equality-only problems, Table IV separately reports AITIP at 39/60 (65.0%) and Psitip at 28/60 (46.7%).

## technique: reverse construction of atomic proof data

what it is: construct a target with a guaranteed proof under sampled conditions by combining Shannon atoms and conditions, then record residual/step pairs as supervised examples. A residual starts at `R0 = Itarget`; at each step a permitted term `Tt >= 0` is subtracted, `Rt+1 = Rt - Tt`, until zero. The authors explain: “Since our training framework is step-wise, each prompt corresponds to the current residual inequality, and the model is expected to output only one proof step at a time.” (Section III-A, p. 2.)

how to apply:
1. sample `n` in 3..9 and a number of atoms (Algorithm 1, p. 3)
2. generate atoms from `H(A)`, `H(A|B)`, `I(A;B)`, and `I(A;B|C)`
3. sample equality and inequality conditions with LP feasibility checking; split equality `C=0` into `C>=0` and `-C>=0` (Algorithm 1)
4. assign positive coefficients to Shannon atoms and inequality conditions, and nonzero signed coefficients to equality conditions; sum them into residual `R` (Algorithm 1)
5. let `Nminus(R)` be the coordinates where `R` is negative; select the remaining weighted candidate `(lambda, v)` maximizing `-sum_{q in Nminus(R)} lambda*v[q]`, format `(R, conditions)` as input and `lambda*v >= 0` as output, subtract it, remove that candidate, and repeat until the multiset is empty (Algorithm 1)

The resulting four datasets each contain 389.5M tokens. Dataset A has 508,803 rows, 4096-token context, and 6.0% `n=9`; B has 256,870 rows, 4096-token context, and 52.6% `n=9`; C and D use 8192 tokens with 8.0% and 66.9% `n=9`, respectively (Section III-D, p. 3). LoRA rank is 64 and alpha is 128, with AdamW 8-bit (Section III-B, pp. 2–3). A pilot checkpoint sweep selects fraction 0.6 of the training schedule, checkpoint 9534, with 24/30 validation successes and an average successful tree size of 385.3; that fraction is then shared across subsequent models (Section III-E, pp. 3–4).

Verus form (Inference): generate verified Rust training pairs by taking a function contract and a known proof, selecting one local lemma or `assert` obligation, and recording the remaining obligation as context. Replace entropy atoms with Verus `proof fn` lemmas, `requires`, `ensures`, and `assert ... by`; accept a training step only after Verus checks it. Rust ownership and mutable borrows mean a residual cannot simply be a vector of coefficients, so this is a representation inference, not a claim from the paper.

Inference: reverse construction guarantees a proof under the sampled conditions, but does not establish that the generated problems represent realistic theorem distributions. Section IV-A (p. 4) says the final test set is generated by Algorithm 1 and obtains n=11–15 samples by randomly permuting indices of n=10 samples to avoid direct LP generation costs. Inference: the final results therefore do not establish performance on independently generated problems with increasing numbers of distinct variables. The paper does not report duplicate removal or project-wise holdouts. Inference: VL should consider crate- and API-level splits to test transfer beyond memorized lemma text.

## technique: guided beam search with verifier-oriented pruning

what it is: at each residual, sample `K` candidate steps, compute each new residual, and retain a beam of size `B` through depth `D`. The score is the sum of absolute negative residual coefficients, `score(c) = sum_{i:ci<0}|ci| * 10^3`; smaller scores are preferred (Section III-C, p. 3). Candidates pass three filters: format validity, rejection of all-nonpositive residuals, and (for `n<=9`) LP false pruning. For the seven-step example, the paper says: “Each step is independently verifiable as a Shannon-type entropy inequality.” (Section II-C, p. 2.)

steps:
1. format-filter only positive scalar multiples of the four permitted atom forms or known conditions (Section III-C, p. 3)
2. subtract each valid candidate; recognize zero as success (Section II-B), and reject all-nonpositive residuals (Section III-C); Inference: that pruning must exclude the successful zero residual
3. when the residual and all conditions together involve at most nine variables, run Shannon-type LP to reject impossible residuals; the implementation disables this at `n>=10` because its LP cannot incorporate all variables and conditions (Section III-C)
4. score survivors by negative-coefficient magnitude and keep the best beam states
5. stop on residual zero or at maximum depth; default search is beam 24, four rollouts, depth 20 (Section IV-A), while the selected hard-set policy is beam 32, six rollouts, depth 24 (Section IV-C, p. 4)

Verus form (Inference): make each candidate a complete Rust/Verus patch and run Verus before inserting it into the beam. Use diagnostic categories as pruning predicates, keep candidates whose verified `assert` or `proof fn` reduces the remaining postcondition obligations, and score by a measurable count of unresolved obligations. Verus `invariant` is a loop annotation, not a function call; the paper's residual arithmetic does not carry over directly.

evidence: on the 30-item hard set, beam 32/rollouts 6/depth 24 proves 16/30 (53.3%); beam 24/4/20 proves 9/30 (30.0%), and beam 64/6/24 proves 10/30 (33.3%) (Table III, p. 4). On the four-dataset ablation, Dataset A scores 9/30 (30%) hard and 12/15 (80%) long; Dataset D scores 8/30 (27%) hard and 9/15 (60%) long (Table II, p. 4). Section IV-D describes an identical beam 32/6/24 comparison for both model sizes, but Table II's 0.6B hard-set result is 9/30, whereas Table III reports 16/30 for that policy. The paper leaves this discrepancy unresolved. Random scoring drops the easy-set result from 25/30 (83.3%) to 7/30 (23.3%), and average successful tree size changes from 1100.3 for greedy scoring to 244.1 for random scoring (Section IV-G, p. 5). These denominators and settings are essential: the 83.3%/23.3% comparison is the 30-item easy set, not the 60-item final test. The authors attribute the smaller random-scoring average to success only on small-tree problems.

limits: the authors report that unsuccessful high-parameter runs terminated naturally without hitting tree or time budgets (Section IV-C, p. 4), while their failure analysis describes weak steps and repeated near-duplicates at aggressive settings (Section V-B, p. 5). DeepSeek V3.2 and GPT-5.4-mini produced 45–300 format-invalid proposals per run when substituted into the search on five n=3 problems; they proved 1/5 and 0/5, respectively (Section IV-F, pp. 5–6). LP pruning is disabled at the larger `n` values in this implementation. Inference: a Verus adaptation should measure verification cost before choosing caching and SMT time limits.

## implications for VL

The paper demonstrates atomic-step supervision, reverse proof-data construction, residual-distance scoring, format and feasibility pruning, and controlled beam ablations. Inference: VL could test these ideas on verified Rust obligations. Inference: a checked proof does not establish that its specification and assumptions express the intended property; a Verus adaptation should combine a final Verus run with specification and assumption review. The paper evaluates entropy inequalities, so it supplies no direct evidence about success rates on Rust programs.

## techniques for the catalog

- reverse proof-data generation — build guaranteed-valid targets and train one atomic proof step against each residual
- residual-reduction search — subtract validated atoms until the residual is exactly zero
- guided beam scoring — rank branches by negative residual magnitude and retain a bounded beam
- format and feasibility pruning — reject malformed steps, impossible residuals, and LP-inconsistent branches before expansion
