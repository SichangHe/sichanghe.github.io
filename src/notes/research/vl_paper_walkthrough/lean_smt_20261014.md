# Lean-SMT: An SMT tactic for discharging proof goals in Lean
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `mohamed2025leansmt`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15796)

## purpose and trust model

Lean-SMT connects Lean goals to the proof-producing SMT solver cvc5. Its abstract states: “We detail how the tactic converts Lean goals into SMT problems and, more importantly, how it reconstructs SMT proofs into native Lean proofs.” (Abstract, p. 1) The tactic aims at automation comparable to Isabelle’s Sledgehammer while preserving a small trusted kernel.

Inference: use it when a Lean goal lies in the supported first-order fragment and SMT reasoning seems useful. The paper’s workflow is: preprocess the dependent-type-theory goal, translate it to SMT-LIB, call cvc5, reconstruct its Cooperating Proof Calculus (CPC) proof into Lean, and let the Lean kernel check the resulting proof (Section 3, p. 4).

## technique: proof-producing preprocessing

Lean’s dependent type theory is more expressive than SMT many-sorted first-order logic. First run `lean-auto`, which normalizes universe levels, monomorphizes definitions, adds lemmas for inductive types, and replaces typeclass instances with values. The paper states: “All of these transformations are implemented in Lean and are proof producing, so their soundness is guaranteed by Lean’s kernel.” It also warns that they are incomplete because of the expressivity gap (Section 3.1, p. 5). Then apply Lean-SMT preprocessing to encode constructs without direct SMT-LIB counterparts, such as `Prop`, `Nat`, `Rat`, and `Iff`.

The paper’s example starts with a group goal: “⊢ ∀ (G : Type u) [Group G] (e : G), (∀ (a : G), e * a = a) ↔ e = 1”. Preprocessing expands the `Group` typeclass into explicit operation and axiom assumptions and changes the bidirectional connective into an equality comparison (Example 1, p. 5). Inference: expose implicit structure before translation and preserve proofs of the transformations.

Inference: a similar design for Verus could make implicit assumptions explicit before translation and retain evidence that preprocessing preserves the goal. The paper does not evaluate Verus.

## technique: sound Lean-to-SMT translation

Translate the preprocessed formula into SMT-LIB and assert the negation whose unsatisfiability establishes the goal. The key soundness hazard is that SMT sorts are nonempty while Lean types may be empty. Lean-SMT lets reconstruction fail if a proof step relies on nonemptiness that Lean cannot establish; an available `Nonempty` instance discharges the mismatch (Section 3.2, pp. 5–6). In the group example, the identity element itself guarantees that `G` is nonempty.

Inference: the workflow can be organized as these practical steps:

1. identify every Lean sort and operation after preprocessing;
2. emit SMT declarations and axioms for those operations;
3. encode the negated target and call `check-sat`;
4. reject a result whose reconstruction would require an unproved nonempty type;
5. retain the correspondence between each translated formula and its Lean proof.

Inference: an analogous integration should state domain assumptions explicitly and require a checked reconstruction before accepting an SMT result as a proof.

## technique: CPC proof reconstruction

cvc5 emits CPC proofs with premises, rule arguments, and optional decidable side conditions. Lean-SMT supports about 200 of more than 662 CPC rules, approximately 30%, prioritizing the fragment common in Lean goals (Section 3.3, p. 6). It reconstructs every supported step into a Lean theorem, tactic, or verified Lean program and submits the complete result to the kernel. Unsupported steps are exposed for manual proof, preserving soundness.

The paper describes three reconstruction forms: theorems cover 163 rules, specialized tactics cover 37 rules using around 400 theorems, and reflection covers five rules (Section 3.3, pp. 6–8). First, encode a rule directly as a theorem. For example, the paper formalizes the arithmetic tangent rule with `arithMulTangentLowerEq` and `arithMulTangentUpperEq` (Example 3, p. 7). Second, use a specialized tactic when a rule has many cases. For `ARITH_SUM_UB`, the implementation uses nine inequality variants of a polymorphic `sumBoundsThm` over `LinearOrderedRing`; this avoids one unwieldy theorem and handles mixed integer and real variables (Example 5, p. 8; Appendix A, pp. 16–17). Mixed integer/real inputs are handled by casting integer inequalities to real inequalities before combining them. Third, use reflection for complex decidable side conditions. The paper reuses `ac_rfl` for associative/commutative normalization and implements `poly_norm` for polynomial expansion. Its correctness proof required around 70 theorems and 620 lines of Lean. For `poly_norm`, the paper states: “Since variables are represented as natural numbers, the premise of the theorem does not contain actual variables.” Lean can establish that premise through definitional equality; compiling its decidable test achieved up to 25 times faster evaluation on very large arithmetic expressions (Section 3.3, pp. 8–9). The paper does not explain the trust implications of the compiled variant.

For disjunction, the paper uses a list representation `orN` and the theorem `orN_resolution`, because CPC treats disjunction as n-ary while Lean’s `Or` is binary (Example 4, p. 7). The theorem removes complementary literals with `eraseIdx` and concatenates the remaining lists. The paper explains: “Using List permits leveraging general theorems about List we proved for eliminating tedious corner cases that would otherwise arise in a Prop implementation.” (Example 4, p. 7)

Inference: a Verus adaptation could investigate theorem-based reconstruction and explicit formula semantics, but the paper provides no implementation or evaluation of that adaptation.

## evidence and limits

The evaluation uses 5,000 Sledgehammer benchmarks from Isabelle and 24,817 unsatisfiable SMT-COMP 2024 benchmarks in UF, IDL, RDL, LIA, LRA, LIRA, and their quantifier-free siblings (Section 4, p. 9). The Isabelle benchmarks include selected premises, with up to 512 lemmas per problem (Section 4.1, p. 10). Table 1 reports 2,868 solved-and-proved, 2,866 checked, and 2,847 checked without holes for cvc5+Lean-SMT; veriT+Sledgehammer checked 2,180 without holes and Duper checked 1,116 (Appendix B, p. 17). Section 4.1 says: “lean-smt takes less than a second to replay proofs for 98% of the benchmarks, with the remaining 2% taking less than 5 seconds.” (p. 10) It does not specify the denominator for those replay percentages; they should not be read as success rates over all 5,000 inputs.

For the SMT-COMP set, cvc5 produced 21,595 proofs for the Lean-SMT path, of which 15,271 (71%) were checked, including proofs with holes; 14,099 were checked without holes (Section 4.2, p. 11; Table 2, p. 17). The paper warns: “On occasion, cvc5 will produce proofs containing holes. Both Ethos and lean-smt verify every non-hole proof step.” (footnote 14, p. 11) Appendix B defines the final column as successful checking where “the proof was complete.” (p. 17) Thus the 71% figure does not measure complete proof verification alone.

Ethos checked 21,196 of 21,541 generated proofs (98%), with 18,018 checked without holes; SMTCoq checked 4,178 complete proofs (Table 2, p. 17). The cvc5 proof counts differ because Ethos receives printed and piped proofs while Lean-SMT receives proofs directly through the API (footnote 13, p. 11). On the quantifier-free subset of 11,804 inputs, Lean-SMT checked 5,091 proofs, including 4,869 without holes; Ethos checked 8,860, including 6,892 without holes; SMTCoq checked 4,178 complete proofs (Table 3, p. 18). Section 4.2 specifies a standard 20-minute timeout (p. 10). The hardware description says: “All benchmarks were executed on a cluster with nodes equipped with 48 AMD Ryzen 9 7950X processors running at 4.50GHz and 128GB of RAM each.” (footnote 10, p. 9) It does not call these 48-core nodes.

The paper says: “lean-smt stays within an order of magnitude of Ethos for most benchmarks.” (Section 4.2, p. 11) For trust, it states that Lean-SMT and SMTCoq “both rely on small kernels” and that SMTCoq’s extraction mechanism “has to be trusted as well.” Ethos additionally trusts the Eunoia signature for CPC; its kernel includes native arithmetic via GMP and arrays, whereas Lean’s kernel only natively supports arithmetic (Section 4.2, p. 11). Lean-SMT’s reconstruction libraries produce proofs checked by Lean’s kernel rather than extending that trusted base. The paper also notes reliance on the axiom of choice for classical reasoning (Section 3.3, p. 6). Bit-vectors, floats, strings, additional proof rules, and broader Lean datatype support are future work (Section 5, p. 12).

Inference: for VL, the transferable idea is flexible solver-proof reconstruction with a small kernel. Any Verus adaptation would need separate validation. The reported coverage and the distinction between proofs with and without holes show why successful reconstruction and completeness must be measured separately; the paper does not isolate the causes of every checking failure.

## techniques for the catalog

- proof-producing preprocessing: normalize rich proof-assistant goals while carrying kernel-checked rewrite proofs
- sound SMT translation: encode a negated goal and check the Lean/SMT nonempty-sort mismatch
- CPC reconstruction: replay solver proof steps as native kernel-checked theorems, tactics, or reflection
- n-ary-resolution encoding: represent disjunctions as lists to match solver semantics
- certificate reflection: verify decidable side conditions through definitional equality; the paper also experiments with compiled evaluation
