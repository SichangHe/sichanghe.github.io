# A Framework for the Verification of Certifying Computations
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `alkassar2013certifying`, [paper](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.7462v1)

## what the framework establishes

Alkassar, Böhme, Mehlhorn, and Rizkallah split verification of a difficult algorithm into a small executable checker and a mathematical witness proof. Their abstract says: “A checker for such a witness is usually much simpler than the original algorithm – yet it is all the user has to trust” (abstract, p. 1). A certifying algorithm returns output `y` and witness `w`; checker `C(x,y,w)` accepts exactly when `w` establishes the postcondition for `y` (Fig. 1, p. 2).

The formal ingredients are a precondition ϕ(x), postcondition ψ(x,y), and witness predicate W(x,y,w) satisfying `ϕ(x) ∧ W(x,y,w) → ψ(x,y)` (§2, p. 3). The framework proves two independent obligations: checker correctness (termination and exact accept/reject behavior under ϕ) and the witness property (the mathematical implication) (§2, pp. 3–4). VCC verifies C code and Isabelle/HOL proves the higher-level witness theorem. The paper states: “We use the automatic verifier VCC for establishing the correctness of the checker and the interactive theorem prover Isabelle/HOL for high-level mathematical properties of algorithms” (abstract, p. 1).

The case studies cover connected components, single-source shortest paths with nonnegative weights, and maximum-cardinality matching in LEDA (§4, pp. 8–27). Only the positive connectivity case (spanning-tree witness) is treated; the cut witness for a disconnected graph is not verified (§4.1, p. 8). The matching formalization found a real checker defect: the authors state that “the checker program in LEDA is incorrect in that it does not check that the matching of a graph is a subset of the edges of the graph” (§1, p. 3; also §5, p. 28).

## technique: design a witness and a complete checker

When to use it. Use the split when the producer algorithm is too complex to verify directly but can emit a compact certificate. The paper’s gcd example returns `g,s,t`; the checker checks that `g` divides `a` and `b` and that `g = s·a + t·b` (§1, p. 2).

Steps from the paper. (1) Define abstract input, output, witness sets X, Y, W and representation maps `iX`, `iY`, `iW` (§2, p. 3). (2) State ϕ and ψ. (3) Define W so that ϕ and W imply ψ; for gcd, W states that `g = sa + tb` and that `g` divides `a` and `b` (§2, p. 4). (4) Implement C over concrete representations. (5) Prove that, when ϕ holds, C halts, accepts if W holds, and rejects otherwise; when ϕ fails, C “may do anything (run forever or halt with an arbitrary output)” (§2, p. 4). Consequently a rejection tells the user only “¬ϕ(x) ∨ ¬W(x, y, w)” (Theorem 1, p. 4). (6) Prove W’s mathematical implication separately (§2, pp. 3–4).

Verus form. Inference: encode the checker as ordinary executable Rust with `requires` for ϕ and `ensures` stating `accepted == W(input, output, witness)`. Define W as a `spec fn`; prove the implication with a `proof fn` or lemma. Use `old(...)` in postconditions when the checker mutates buffers. This is an inference because the paper uses VCC and Isabelle, not Verus.

## technique: separate low-level code proof from high-level mathematics

VCC annotations establish W as the checker’s postcondition and define ϕ, ψ, W, and representation maps with ghost types/functions (§2, pp. 5–6). The framework exports these pure mathematical objects to Isabelle. The translation is syntactic because the authors restrict the interface to a common second-order fragment (§2, p. 6). Isabelle proves the witness theorem in an abstract graph library, while linking lemmas connect concrete VCC representations to abstract graphs (§2, p. 6; §4.1.3).

Steps. (1) Annotate C data structures and functions until VCC proves termination and the checker postcondition (the paper proves total correctness, §1, p. 3). (2) Define ghost maps from concrete arrays and records to mathematical sets, graphs, paths, or labelings. (3) Export the precondition, witness predicate, postcondition, and abstract representations. (4) Prove the high-level theorem in Isabelle/HOL. (5) Prove linking lemmas and add the theorem back as a VCC axiom; the paper explains this is sound because the interface is expressible equivalently in Isabelle (§2, p. 6). The paper translated manually from VCC to Isabelle/HOL and says the translation “can be automated” (§5, p. 28). Inference: a Verus replacement would keep executable checking and ghost mathematical predicates in one Rust crate, while treating the abstract theorem as a proved lemma rather than an unchecked axiom.

The paper emphasizes usability: “The user of a verified checker only has to look at its VCC specification” (§2, p. 6). Inference: this holds only if the translation to Isabelle and the VCC axiom for the witness property are sound, which the paper argues in §2, p. 6.

## technique: instantiate graph witnesses

For connected components, the witness is a spanning tree given as a root `r` and arrays `parent_edge` and `num`. The checker is two C functions: `check_r` tests that `r` is a vertex with `num[r] == 0` and `parent_edge[r] == -1`, and `check_parent_num` tests for every other vertex `v` that `parent_edge[v]` is an edge incident to `v` whose other endpoint has `num` one smaller than `num[v]` (§4.1.1, pp. 9–10, Listing 2). The postcondition is that any two vertices are joined by a path (§4.1.2, p. 12). The Isabelle proof is an induction on `num v` showing every vertex is reachable from `r`, in a locale over Noschinski’s graph library (§4.1.3, pp. 12–13). For shortest paths with nonnegative weights, the witness is a shortest-path tree encoded by `parent_edge`, `num` and `dist`; the output is `dist`. The checker has four functions, `check_start_val`, `check_no_path`, `check_trian` (triangle inequality on all edges) and `check_just` (the parent edge of each reachable vertex justifies its `dist` and `num` values) (§4.2, pp. 14–16, Listing 5). The `num` depth function is needed because zero-cost edges are allowed (§4.2, pp. 14–15). Isabelle proves `dist v ≤ µ c s v` and `dist v ≥ µ c s v` in separate lemmas and combines them in the locale `shortest-path-pos-cost` (§4.2.3, pp. 19–20). The paper handles C’s finite arithmetic by case distinctions on infinity and by assuming `int` is 32-bit and casting nonnegative values to unsigned (§4.2.1, p. 15). For matching, the output is a matching `M` and the witness is an odd-set cover `L` (a vertex labeling), plus an array `f` mapping matching edges to input edges. The checker has `check_subset`, `check_matching`, `check_osc`, and `weight`, and succeeds if the first three succeed and `weight` equals the number of edges of `M` (§4.3.1, pp. 21–23, Listing 10). Isabelle proves Edmonds’ Theorem 2, that `|M| = n1 + Σ⌊ni/2⌋` for an odd-set cover implies maximum cardinality (§4.3, p. 21; §4.3.3, pp. 26–27). The paper calls proving absence of overflow in `weight` “much harder to establish than functional correctness” (§4.3.2, p. 26).

Inference: the reusable design is to choose a certificate whose checker consists of local, bounded loops, then reserve global optimality or graph-theoretic reasoning for a `proof fn` over mathematical sets. A Verus checker can maintain loop `invariant`s for processed vertices, edge membership, and label inequalities, with `ensures` exposing the witness predicate.

## evidence, trust boundary, and discrepancies

The authors state that “All described theorems and lemmas have been formally verified using VCC and Isabelle/HOL” and that they now prove total correctness and an if-and-only-if accept/reject property for the three checkers (§1, p. 3; §5, p. 28). Table 1 gives exact line counts: connected components 61 C lines, 162 VCC annotation lines, 63 exported Isabelle lines, 102 locale lines, and 163 linking lines; shortest paths 107, 318, 109, 279, 198; matching 124, 263, 78, 318, 164 (Table 1, p. 29). The paper reports an average ratio of 2.5 for the VCC annotation overhead and 6.7 when Isabelle proofs are included, an overall VCC proof time of 10 seconds for all checkers, and an overall Isabelle proof time “well below one minute” on a 2.9 GHz Intel Core i7 (§5, p. 28). The paper does not define what the ratios are relative to and gives no per-checker times; I did not reproduce the ratios from Table 1.

The authors explicitly do not verify the producer algorithms: “we did not prove the correctness of the original programs but only verified the results of their computations” (§7, p. 31). The paper notes the producer may be incorrect and “does not always provide a y and w such that the checker accepts” (§2, p. 6). The verified code is C; the paper says “Verifying the C++ implementations remains open” (§2, p. 4). Isabelle’s correctness rests on its inference kernel (§3, pp. 6–7). Inference: the trusted base also includes VCC, the representation maps, the checker specification, the manual VCC-to-Isabelle translation, and the assumption that the producer’s output is supplied to C; the paper does not list a trusted base.

## techniques for the catalog

- witness-checker split — emit a certificate and verify a small checker instead of the complex producer
- checker iff contract — prove termination and exact acceptance/rejection under the input precondition
- two-level proof interface — verify concrete code with contracts and prove abstract mathematics in a theorem prover
- representation linking — connect concrete arrays and records to abstract graphs, sets, paths, or labelings
- local graph certificates — use spanning trees, distance labels, or matching covers whose global meaning is proved separately
