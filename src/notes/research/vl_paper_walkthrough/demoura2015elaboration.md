# Elaboration in Dependent Type Theory
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `demoura2015elaboration`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04324)

## scope and result
De Moura, Avigad, Kong, and Roux describe Lean’s elaborator: a procedure that turns partially specified dependent-type expressions into fully typed terms. The abstract says: “Lean’s elaborator supports higher-order unification, type class inference, ad hoc overloading, insertion of coercions, the use of tactics, and the computational reduction of terms.” (Abstract, p. 1). The abstract also says that “the elaboration algorithm has been carefully designed to balance efficiency and usability.” The central engineering problem is therefore balancing inference power, informative errors, and speed when these mechanisms interact. The paper gives pseudocode and prose for the algorithm; it states no soundness or completeness theorem.

## technique 1: holes, constraints, and two-phase elaboration
What it is. Parsing produces a preterm. Preprocessing replaces omitted information with metavariables and emits unification or choice constraints. Constraint resolution then solves them, invokes tactics for remaining holes, and reports justified failures. The paper states: “The process for getting from a preterm to a fully elaborated term has two main steps: preprocessing and constraint resolution.” (Section 3.1, pp. 12–13). It adds that the split is “slightly too simplistic”, because preprocessing also simplifies constraints to decide whether to insert a coercion (Section 3.1, p. 13).

How to apply it. (1) Represent each omitted argument or type with a fresh metavariable applied to the variables of the local context; the paper encodes a hole in context `(x : A) (y : B)` as `?m x y` (Section 3.2, p. 14; Section 3.5, p. 20). (2) Generate unification constraints for type compatibility and choice constraints for overloads, coercions, and type classes (Sections 3.1 and 3.2, pp. 13, 15). Coercions and type classes use “ondemand” choice constraints that run only after the metavariables in the expected type are assigned (Section 3.2, pp. 15–16; Section 3.5, p. 20). (3) Solve “straightforward” constraints first so their assignments guide later search; the solver orders its queue by constraint category (Section 3.1, p. 13; Section 3.6, p. 21). (4) Keep constraints in a priority queue, and on each non-pattern constraint create a case split whose remaining alternatives are a lazy list; backtrack to a case split when a branch fails (Section 3.6, pp. 20–22). (5) Invoke tactic blocks after all constraints are solved; nested tactic preterms recurse through the same pipeline (Section 3.1, p. 13).

Verus form.

Evidence and limits. The paper says the Lean kernel ignores the reducibility annotations when it type-checks fully elaborated definitions (Section 3.3, p. 16). Inference: elaboration heuristics therefore affect usability and search, not the kernel’s final type judgment. The paper notes that “Even second-order unification is known to be generally undecidable” (Section 2.2, p. 4) and that its search for flex-rigid solutions is “incomplete” and has “a threshold on the number of steps” (Section 3.7, pp. 22–23).

## technique 2: higher-order unification with nonchronological backtracking
What it is. Dependent proofs require inferring functions such as the predicate in `subst` or in induction (Section 2.2, p. 4). The unifier handles quasi-pattern and flex-rigid constraints with a variation of Huet’s algorithm, using imitation and projection case splits (Section 3.7, pp. 22–23). It records a justification for every constraint, and on failure jumps back to the case split whose assumption the failure depends on, rather than undoing choices chronologically (Section 3.6, p. 22). There are three kinds of justification: asserted, assumption, and join (Section 3.2, p. 15).

How to apply it. (1) Store each metavariable’s type; because only closed terms are assigned to metavariables, the metavariable is applied to the variables of its context (Section 3.2, p. 14). (2) Classify the constraints that `simp` returns as delta, pattern, quasi-pattern, flex-rigid, flex-flex, or recursor (Section 3.4, p. 19). Pattern constraints are solved immediately and never queued (Section 3.6, p. 21). (3) For flex-rigid and quasi-pattern constraints, try projection case splits first “because they generate more general solutions”; for quasi-patterns whose head is a constant not marked reducible, no projection need be considered (Section 3.7, p. 23). Use imitation when the head is the rigid term’s constant, with an extra imitation step after weak head normalization for reducible constants (Section 3.7, p. 23). (4) Keep a stack of case splits, each holding the saved solver state, a fresh assumption justification, the justification of the constraint, and the lazy list of remaining alternatives; the solver state is copied cheaply because it uses red-black trees (Section 3.6, pp. 21–22). (5) On error, `resolve` pops case splits until it finds one whose assumption the error’s justification depends on, restores its state, and pulls the next alternative; if none remains, elaboration fails (Section 3.6, p. 22). The paper’s flex-rigid solution template is `λx1 … xn, h (?m1 x1 … xn) … (?mp x1 … xn)` (Section 3.7, p. 22).

Verus form. Inference: use a bounded candidate search over lemma instantiations and `assert` forms, retaining the Verus diagnostic that identifies the failed obligation. A candidate branch may add a `requires` fact or invoke a `proof fn`; submit each branch to Verus. A tool can backtrack nonchronologically by dependency, but the proof accepted by Verus remains the evidence.

Evidence and limits. The paper says it ignores the case where the head `h` is a recursor, “since the search space would become too big if we considered recursors for h”, and that even with nonchronological backtracking the extra imitation step cannot be tried for all constants because “the search space becomes too big” (Section 3.7, p. 23). It also has a threshold on the number of solver steps (Section 3.7, p. 23). It reports that removing an older recursor case-split feature broke only three library theorems, all fixable by giving implicit arguments explicitly (Section 3.7, p. 24). Inference: completeness is not claimed.

## technique 3: controlled computation and unfolding
What it is. Elaboration reduces beta/iota redexes (`reduceβι`) and selectively unfolds definitions. Definitions are marked irreducible, reducible, or semireducible; an unannotated definition is semireducible (Section 3.3, p. 16). The paper warns: “Determining when to unfold defined constants is a crucial part of the practice of theorem proving.” (Section 2.3, p. 6). An irreducible definition is never unfolded during higher-order unification (but can be during type checking); a reducible one is always eligible; a semireducible one can be unfolded during simple decisions and not during complex ones (Section 2.3, p. 6). A decision is simple if unfolding does not require an extra case split (Section 3.3, p. 16). During type class resolution, `whnf` does not unfold semireducible definitions (Section 3.3, p. 17).

How to apply it. (1) Normalize beta and iota redexes in `simp` (Section 3.4, p. 18). (2) For a delta constraint `f s ≈ f t`, first assume `f` is opaque and solve the arguments pairwise. (3) If that fails, unfold `f` on both sides and retry; the paper calls this case split “a heuristic optimization” that “is not necessary for completeness” (Section 3.7, p. 22). (4) Respect user annotations and do not unfold every definition: the naive approach of performing all unfoldings “leads to unacceptable performance” (Section 2.3, p. 6). (5) Keep these hints out of kernel checking; they only guide elaboration (Sections 2.3 and 3.3, pp. 6, 16).

Verus form.

## technique 4: integrated type classes, overloads, coercions, and tactics
Type-class inference solves implicit structures: for a generic multiplication, the elaborator must solve `?M : has_mul nat`, and it finds `nat_has_mul` on a stored list of instances (Section 2.4, p. 7). Instances with implicit class arguments are handled by “a backward-chaining Prolog-like search” (Section 2.4, p. 7); Section 3.5 (p. 20) describes the procedure as essentially a simple λ-Prolog interpreter whose Horn clauses are the user-declared instances. Ad hoc overloads add “ambiguity and choice points” (Section 2.5, p. 10) and are realized as regular choice constraints (Section 3.5, p. 20). The paper describes three kinds of coercion: between families of types, from a family of types to sorts, and from a family of types to function types (Section 2.6, p. 10). During preprocessing, if the type of an argument is not convertible to the expected type, the preprocessor looks for a coercion in a database of coercions and inserts it (Section 3.5, pp. 19–20). Tactics fill terms that unification and type class resolution cannot synthesize; tactic blocks and explicit terms can be mixed, and `proof ... qed` is sugar for `by+ exact` that makes the elaborator process the enclosing expression separately from the proof, so a long proof can be treated as several smaller elaboration problems (Section 2.7, pp. 10–11).

Verus form. Inference: map Rust traits and explicit module contracts to a restricted registry of candidate lemmas and specifications. Generate explicit trait bounds and proof calls rather than relying on undocumented coercions. Split long generated proofs into small `proof fn`s, each checked by Verus, and preserve the executable/ghost boundary.

## what is proved, measured, and trusted
The paper describes an implemented algorithm and gives no theorem. Its evidence is experience with Lean libraries: “Our procedure has been tested with the development of more than 65k lines of Lean’s formal library, and the experience has shown that it provides powerful and effective support for the formalization process.” (Section 4, p. 26). Section 4 (p. 24) gives about 42k lines for the standard library and more than 25k for the homotopy type theory library. The one timing in the paper is for the locally nameless bound-index optimization, not for the elaboration heuristics: the 172-file, 41,700-line standard library compiles in 71.06 seconds with the optimizations and 2,189.97 seconds without (Section 3.3, p. 18). This is engineering evidence, not a theorem of completeness or a benchmark denominator of successful elaborations.

## techniques for the catalog
- hole-and-constraint elaboration — replace omissions with metavariables, generate constraints, simplify, solve, then invoke tactics
- dependency-aware backtracking — track justifications and jump to the choice responsible for a failed branch
- selective reduction — always reduce beta/iota redexes, and control delta unfolding with reducibility hints to contain search
- integrated proof inference — coordinate type classes, overloads, coercions, tactics, and proof chunking
