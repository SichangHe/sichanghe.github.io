# Beyond Gold Standards: Epistemic Ensemble of LLM Judges for Formal Mathematical Reasoning
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `zhang2025gold`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10903v2)

## purpose and setting
The paper evaluates statement autoformalizations without requiring a reference formalization. It separates alignment with the natural-language statement from intrinsic formal-code quality and syntax. The authors state: “We break down the components involved within the context of autoformalization tasks and organize them into a taxonomy composed of four core aspects: logical preservation (LP), mathematical consistency (MC), formal quality (FQ), and formal validity (FV), along with a set of atomic properties that serve as computable estimators of these features.” LP, MC, and FQ are assessed by LLM judges; FV is checked by theorem provers (section 1, page 2).

## technique: three-tier evaluation taxonomy
What it is. Tier 1 distinguishes Alignment Faithfulness (AF), which concerns the intended natural-language semantics, from Formalization Correctness (FC), which concerns validity, naturalness, and well-formedness in the target formalism. Tier 2 decomposes AF into LP and MC and FC into FQ and FV. Tier 3 operationalizes these aspects with nine properties: Pre-arg Structure, Quantification, Formula, Relation, Concept, Constant, Operator, Conciseness, and Logical Consistency (section 2.2, pages 3–4). For example, Appendix B defines Quantification: “ϕ accurately formalizes all quantifiers, such as universal and existential, present in s.” (page 11). Figure 1 groups the first four properties under LP, the next three under MC, and the final two under FQ (page 2).

When to use it. Inference: use this when a prover pass/fail result supplies too little information about a translation. The paper illustrates a minimum-value statement formalized only as a lower bound, a missing negation in an infimum identity, and unnecessary variables and hypotheses in an otherwise correct Lean theorem (Table 1 and section 2.1, page 3). For the first case, it explains that the formalization omits “the critical information that the minimum is actually attained for some value of x.”

How to apply it. Identify source statement s and candidate formalization ϕ. In direct Tier-2 assessment (DA-T2), obtain LP, MC, and FQ judgments from the LLM and FV from the prover, then average the four scores. In OAP assessment, ask the LLM independently about the nine atomic properties, average the properties within each of LP, MC, and FQ with equal within-aspect weights, insert prover FV, and average the four Tier-2 scores (section 3.1.1 and Table 2, pages 4–5). The more general metric is S = wLP SLP + wMC SMC + wFQ SFQ + wFV SFV, with scores in [0, 1] and positive weights summing to one (section 2.2, page 4). The authors clarify: “The "ensemble" term used in this paper refers to using the same backend LLM to assess different aspects, unlike setups with multiple backend LLMs judging the same aspect.” (footnote 2, page 2).

Verus form. Inference: adapt the aspect questions to a Rust candidate and its specification, and collect Verus feedback separately. Ask whether the specification preserves the natural-language quantifiers, relations, objects, and operations, then inspect conciseness and internal consistency. Verus acceptance would concern the encoded program and specification; the paper's FV concerns syntactic validity of a formalized mathematical statement. These are different checks. Inference: this adaptation offers structured review of requirements, not proof that the specification expresses them faithfully. The paper evaluates Lean4 and Isabelle/HOL, not Verus.

Evidence. Section 3.1 samples 50 miniF2F and 50 ProofNet validation statements. Two evaluators independently rank three formalizations per statement: human-written ground truth, GPT-4.1 zero-shot, and Qwen2.5-7B few-shot with eight exemplars (page 4). It reports: “The EM and PM scores are 38.00% and 70.00% on miniF2F, and 22.00% and 68.67% on ProofNet.” EM means exact ranking match; PM means pairwise ranking match. Thus each dataset has 50 statement rankings, with three formalization pairs per statement, rather than 100 rankings per dataset.

Across five judge models, two datasets, and two human comparison columns, the authors report that OAP achieves the highest exact-match score in 12 cases and the highest pairwise-match score in 17 of 20 cases (section 3.1.1, page 5). Table 2's highest miniF2F PM score is 72.67%, for GPT-4.1-mini OAP against H1; its H2 score is 64.00%. On ProofNet, that judge scores 69.33% against each annotator. GPT-5.4 OAP's 75.33% is a ProofNet H1 score, not a miniF2F score (Table 2, page 5). Inference: these comparisons support agreement with the sampled annotators, not independently established semantic correctness.

Limits and assumptions. FV concerns syntax: “A formally valid expression conforms to all grammatical and structural rules of the formal language.” (section 2.2, page 4). Ranking uses two annotators, whereas the aspect-contribution study uses one expert (sections 3.1–3.2, pages 4–6). The unnumbered Limitations section notes subjective bias, small annotation scale, and that the properties and models are not exhaustive (page 9). Appendix C discusses incorrect judgments and overlapping logical and mathematical aspects (page 11; examples on pages 12–14).

## technique: prover plus fine-grained judge protocol
Table 11 requests a binary judgment for an aspect and a detailed explanation (Appendix A, page 15). Overall assessment is a separate evaluation: Table 6 gives the five-point human annotation scale, from “5: Perfect formalization – fully accurate and complete with no issues.” to “1: Poor formalization – completely fails to reflect the intended meaning of the natural language statement.” (page 12). OA-TP asks the LLM for an overall score using those criteria with detailed prover error feedback; OAP instead computes its final score by averaging aspect scores (section 3.1.1, pages 4–5). Inference: a Verus adaptation could use judge explanations to inspect likely mistranslations while using Verus diagnostics to inspect the encoded verification problem.

## technique: bias and temperature checks
Section 3.3.1 compares GPT-4.1-mini and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B as judges of GPT-4.1 and Qwen2.5-7B few-shot formalizations on miniF2F. Qwen2.5-Coder-7B generally gives lower scores but still rates GPT-4.1 outputs higher across Lean4 and Isabelle/HOL (Table 5, page 8). The authors state: “At present, we find no evidence that the LLM-as-judges framework favors autoformalizations generated by the same model series.” Inference: this limited comparison does not exclude such bias elsewhere.

For randomness, the authors select 50 Isabelle/HOL formalizations produced by Qwen2.5-7B with eight-shot prompting and run GPT-4.1-mini OAP-EW three times at each temperature 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 (section 3.3.2, page 8). They report: “While LP, MC, and FQ scores exhibit some variance across multiple runs, the OA scores show consistent stability.” Inference: this checks score stability on one sample and judge, not stability across arbitrary models or tasks.

## what is proved, measured, and trusted
The paper's prover check establishes FV, defined here as syntactic validity. The experiments measure ranking agreement, aspect correlations, model comparisons, and temperature variation. Inference: a high ensemble score remains an assessment by learned judges; it does not establish faithful translation. The reported human disagreement and incorrect-judgment examples further limit treating either a single annotator or a judge as an unquestionable reference.

## techniques for the catalog
- three-tier taxonomy: distinguish AF/FC, then LP/MC/FQ/FV, then nine atomic properties
- OAP ensemble scoring: independently judge atomic properties, average within aspects, and combine with prover validity
- prover-plus-judge protocol: collect aspect judgments and explanations; distinguish five-point overall assessment from computed ensemble scores
- temperature and bias checks: repeat judgments across temperatures and compare judge/autoformalizer model pairs
