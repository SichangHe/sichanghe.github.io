# Verified Approximation Algorithms
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `essmann2022verified`, [paper](https://doi.org/10.46298/lmcs-18(1:36)2022)

## scope and result

Essmann, Nipkow, Robillard, and Sulejmani formalize six approximation algorithms in Isabelle/HOL: vertex cover, independent set, load balancing, center selection, set cover, and bin packing. The abstract states: “We present the first formal verification of approximation algorithms for NP-complete optimization problems” (abstract, p. 1). Their method is uniform: “All proofs are uniformly invariant based.” (abstract, p. 1). Three of the algorithms “had been verified on paper by program verification experts [BM03, BR03]. We found that their claimed invariants need additional conjuncts before they are strong enough to be real invariants” (§1, p. 1), so the prior proofs were pen-and-paper, not formal. For Wei's independent-set algorithm, the earlier invariant “is sufficient to show that the algorithm has an approximation ratio of ∆ + 1. We managed to simplify their argument significantly which led to an improved approximation ratio of ∆” (§1, p. 2). The six ratios are: k (rank-k hypergraph vertex cover), Δ (independent set), 3/2 sorted and 2 unsorted (load balancing), 2 (center selection), H(d*) (set cover), 3/2 (bin packing) (§1, p. 1). The paper proves partial correctness of simple imperative WHILE programs, conditional on mathematical preconditions. Inference: it does not address executable Rust or the soundness of a verifier, since it never discusses either.

## technique 1: Hoare programs with inductive loop invariants

Isabelle's Hoare layer expresses `{P} c {Q}` as partial correctness: “if execution of c starting in a state that satisfies P terminates, then the final state satisfies Q” (Isabelle/HOL, §2, p. 2). The paper states that its proofs “employ a verification condition generator and essentially reduce to showing the preservation of the invariants” (§2, p. 2). Their example is `{m = 0 ∧ p = 0} WHILE m ≠ a INV {p = m*b} DO p := p+b; m := m+1 OD {p = a*b}` (§2, p. 2).

Inference (standard Hoare reasoning plus the structure of §§3–5, not a recipe stated in the paper): a loop is verified by showing initialization establishes the invariant, each body step preserves it, and invariant plus negated loop test implies the postcondition. A workable order is: (1) state a mathematical locale containing finiteness and input assumptions (the paper uses an Isabelle locale in every proof, §3, p. 3); (2) write the WHILE algorithm; (3) define a predicate containing functional facts and approximation bookkeeping; (4) prove initialization; (5) prove preservation as a lemma for arbitrary loop states (e.g. Lemma 3.1, Lemma 4.1, Lemma 5.1); (6) prove the postcondition and ratio from the invariant at loop exit (e.g. Lemma 4.2, Lemma 7.2).

Inference: In Verus, write executable Rust, a `requires` precondition, an `ensures` postcondition, and a loop `invariant` clause. Use `proof fn` lemmas for arithmetic and set facts; keep proof-only state in ghost variables or quantified invariant facts. Verus's `invariant` is syntax attached to a loop, not a callable function. This is an inference because the paper uses Isabelle rather than Verus.

## technique 2: strengthen invariants until preservation is true

The vertex-cover algorithm maintains a set `C` and uncovered edges `F`: choose an arbitrary edge (`some F`), add its vertices to `C`, then remove every edge of `F` that intersects the chosen edge. The invariant `invar C F` is `F ⊆ E ∧ vc (E − F) C ∧ finite C ∧ (∃ M. inv_matching C F M)`, where `inv_matching` says `M` is a matching, `M ⊆ E`, `|C| ≤ k*|M|`, and every edge of `M` is disjoint from every edge of `F` (vertex cover, §3, p. 3). Lemma 3.1 states that `invar` is preserved. The authors report: “Our invariant is stronger than the one in [BM03] which lacks F ⊆ E. Indeed, without this property, the claimed invariant is not invariant (as acknowledged by Müller-Olm).” (§3, p. 3). The same pattern appears for bin packing: Berghammer and Reuter's invariant is `bp (P1 ∪ [[B1]] ∪ P2 ∪ [[B2]] ∪ {{v} | v ∈ V})`, about which the paper says “As it turns out, this invariant is too weak” and adds four conjuncts (disjointness of V from the bins, distinctness of B1 and B2 from the other bins, and disjointness of the two partial solutions) (§8, p. 17).

Apply the pattern by testing every claimed conjunct against one loop iteration. If preservation fails, add the missing relation, prove initialization, then prove the strengthened preservation lemma. The matching witness `M` connects program state to the lower bound `|M| ≤ |C'|` for every finite vertex cover `C'`, which yields the `k` approximation postcondition (§3, p. 3). Inference: In Verus, represent finite sets with the project's verified set abstraction and put closure facts in `invariant`; use `ensures` to expose the final cover and ratio. Inference: do not weaken an invariant merely to satisfy the solver; both cases above show that a plausible published invariant can be too weak or not invariant.

## technique 3: existential ghost bookkeeping

For Wei's independent-set algorithm, executable state is `S` (selected vertices) and `X` (vertices already handled: each iteration picks `x` from `V − X`, adds it to `S`, and adds `x` and its neighbors to `X`). The invariant `∃P. inv_partition S X P` contains `inv_iv S X` (functional correctness) and says `⋃P = X`, each `p ∈ P` equals `{s} ∪ neighbors s` for some vertex `s`, `|P| = |S|`, and `P` is finite (independent set, §4, p. 4). `P` is a cover of `X` rather than a strict partition: the paper calls it “an over-approximation, since some vertices in neighbors x may have been added to X in a previous iteration” (§4, p. 4). The paper says the proof “relies on an auxiliary variable P, which is not needed for the execution of the algorithm, but is used for bookkeeping in the proof” (§4, pp. 3–4). At each iteration (Lemma 4.1), instantiate the successor witness with `P ∪ {neighbors x ∪ {x}}`; at exit (Lemma 4.2), any independent set `S'` contains at most Δ vertices from each `p ∈ P`, so `|S'| ≤ |P| * Δ = |S| * Δ` (§4, p. 4).

Inference: Verus can encode `P` as a ghost map/set in the loop invariant and existentially quantify it when a concrete ghost variable would burden executable code. A `proof fn` can construct the next witness and prove cardinality bounds; it is erased and must not be called for runtime behavior. The paper also describes a simpler variant that records the largest block size `r` in a program variable, with invariant `inv_iv S X ∧ |X| ≤ |S| * r`, and no variable `P`; it gives `|S'| ≤ |S| * r` but is not enough for the Δ ratio “because the argument relies on a global property of the graph” (§4, p. 5). Choose the variant when a global graph fact is not needed.

## technique 4: derive approximation ratios from lower bounds

For sorted load balancing, the program assigns each next job to the machine with minimum load (`min_arg`). The invariant `inv_2` includes `lb T A j` (a valid partial assignment), `j ≤ n`, the ratio bound `makespan T ≤ 3/2 * makespan T'` against every partial solution `T'` for `j` jobs, `∀x > j. T x = 0`, and `j ≤ m → makespan T = Max' (t ‘ {1..j})` (load balancing, §5, p. 7). The proof builds lower bounds: average load (Lemma 5.2), largest job (Lemma 5.3), and, when `m < j` and jobs are sorted, `2*t(j) ≤ makespan` (Lemma 5.4), all on p. 7. Lemma 5.7 relates a solution for `j+1` jobs to one for `j` jobs with no larger makespan. The preservation proof splits on `j+1 ≤ m` (trivially optimal) versus `j+1 > m`, bounds `T(i)` by the average load, and bounds `t(j+1)` by half the makespan, giving `makespan T_g ≤ makespan T1 + makespan T1 / 2 = 3/2 * makespan T1` (§5, pp. 8–9). The unsorted case gives ratio 2 with the simpler invariant `inv_1` and Lemma 5.3 in place of Lemma 5.4 (§5, p. 9).

Inference: In Verus, state each lower bound as a separate lemma with `requires` matching the invariant and `ensures` for the inequality, then call those lemmas in the loop-preservation proof. This modularizes arithmetic and makes denominator/ordering assumptions explicit.

## evidence, trust, and limits

The verified artifacts cover six algorithms and their claimed ratios; the paper reports no runtime benchmark, because the result is proof completion, not empirical performance. On running time the paper says only “The polynomial running time of the approximation algorithm is easy to see in each case.” (§1, p. 2); the proofs are partial correctness. The proofs are published in the Archive of Formal Proofs ([ENRS20]). The independent-set heuristic with ratio `(Δ+2)/3` of Halldórsson and Radhakrishnan is left beyond scope because formalizing it “would require an inductive invariant to keep track of the sequences of values of X and x at each iteration” and relies on case analysis for different types of graphs (§4, p. 5).

Inference: the results rely on Isabelle/HOL's semantics, libraries, verification-condition generator, and underlying prover; the paper does not discuss this trust base.

## techniques for the catalog

- invariant-based Hoare verification — express imperative algorithms with preconditions, postconditions, and loop invariants, then discharge initialization, preservation, and exit obligations
- invariant strengthening — add missing state relations such as `F ⊆ E` when a claimed invariant is not preserved
- existential ghost witnesses — quantify proof-only partitions or matchings to connect executable state to approximation bounds
- lower-bound decomposition — prove average, maximum-item, and structural lower bounds separately before deriving the approximation ratio
- verus translation (inference) — map contracts and ghost invariants to Verus `requires`, `ensures`, loop `invariant`, and erased `proof fn` lemmas
