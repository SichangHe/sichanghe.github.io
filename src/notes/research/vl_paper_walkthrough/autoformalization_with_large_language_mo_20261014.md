# Autoformalization with Large Language Models(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)Tag: `wu2022autoformalization`, [paper](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2205.12615)## scope and resultThis paper studies large language models (LLMs) translating natural-language mathematics into Isabelle/HOL theorem statements, then using the verified statements as training data for a neural theorem prover. Autoformalization is defined as translation from informal mathematics to formal specifications and proofs. The abstract reports, “we show large language models provide new prospects towards this goal.” (Abstract, p. 1.) The main evidence is on mathematical-competition problems, not Rust programs or Verus specifications.The paper reports two distinct outcomes. In a human inspection of 150 random MATH problems, Codex produced 38 perfect formalizations, or 38/150 = 25.3% (Section 4.4, p. 6). In a theorem-proving pipeline, two rounds of expert iteration raised MiniF2F test success from the base model's 29.9% to 35.2% (Table 3, p. 8). These denominators and splits matter: the 25.3% is correctness of translations on 150 sampled problems, while 35.2% is the fraction of MiniF2F test theorems proved by a neural prover.## technique: few-shot autoformalizationwhen to useUse few-shot prompting when the target prover's syntax and library names are known but aligned natural-language/formal data are scarce. The prompt gives a small number of natural-language statements paired with Isabelle theorem statements, then asks the model to translate a new statement.how the paper does it1. Choose two mathematical statements and their Isabelle formalizations as prompt examples (Section 4, pp. 3–4).2. Append the target in the fixed format: `Natural language version: $Natural_Language_Statement. Translate the natural language version to an Isabelle version:` (Section 4, p. 4).3. For “find the answer” questions, append the answer to turn the task into a proposition. The paper writes, “To transform a question into a proposition, we append the final answer after the question:” (Section 4, p. 3), then uses “The final answer is $Answer.”4. Decode greedily: temperature 0 and `p = 1`; the paper performs no prompt engineering or tuning in the case studies (Section 4.2, p. 4).5. Submit the generated theorem to Isabelle syntax and proof checking. A syntactically correct statement can still be semantically wrong, so inspect correctness against the natural-language problem.Figure 1 shows the method on an IMO problem. Codex generates an Isabelle theorem with `fixes f :: "nat => nat"`, assumes `forall n. f (f n) = n + 1987`, and aims to show `False`, correctly encoding proof by contradiction (Figure 1, p. 2).
