# Property Preserving Abstractions for the Verification of Concurrent Systems
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `loiseaux1995property`, [paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf01384313)

## scope and result
The paper gives a property-preserving abstraction method for reactive transition systems. Its abstraction is a Galois connection between concrete and abstract state sets, coupled to a simulation condition. A verified property on the abstract system implies the corresponding property on the concrete system for the stated logic fragment. The paper also gives a computable abstract transition relation and compositional rules for parallel systems.

The central claim is stated in the abstract: “They can be used to verify a property for a system by verifying the same property on a simpler system which is an abstraction of it.” (abstract, p. 11). The paper models a transition system as `S = (Q,R)` and uses `pre` and `post` predicate transformers. A connection `(α,γ)` is a pair of monotonic functions satisfying `Id ⊆ γ∘α` and `α∘γ ⊆ Id` (Definition 3, p. 15). Inference: a Verus development can use a finite abstract datatype and a relation predicate between concrete and abstract states, but Verus will prove the refinement lemmas rather than run the paper's model checker.

## technique 1: Galois-connection simulation

What it is. Define an abstraction map `α : 2^Q1 -> 2^Q2` and concretization `γ : 2^Q2 -> 2^Q1`, then require `α ∘ pre[R1] ∘ γ ⊆ pre[R2]` (Definition 4, p. 17). The paper proves this is equivalent to Milner-style relational simulation through a relation `p ⊆ Q1 × Q2`: take `α = post[p]` and `γ` the dual of `pre[p]` (Propositions 9–10, pp. 17–18). Definition 5 (p. 17) states the relational form as `R1^{-1} p ⊆ p R2^{-1}`.

When to use it. Use it when the concrete state graph is too large but a smaller state space preserves the property fragment of interest. The paper's method is for formulas in fragments of the branching-time μ-calculus. Section 5 (pp. 22–24) defines preservation and proves Theorem 1 (preservation in both directions gives strong preservation); the preservation of the μ-calculus fragments is Theorem 2 in Section 6.2 (pp. 27–28).

How to do it.
1. Choose concrete states `Q`, abstract states `QA`, and relation `p ⊆ Q × QA` (Section 4, p. 18).
2. Check that `p` is total on concrete states. Construct `Rp = p^{-1} R p` and `Sp=(QA,Rp)` (Notation 3 and Proposition 11, p. 19).
3. Establish the simulation condition: `S ⊑p SA` iff `post[p] ∘ pre[R] ∘ dual(pre[p]) ⊆ pre[RA]` (Section 6.3, p. 30), equivalently `R1^{-1} p ⊆ p R2^{-1}` for the two systems (Definition 5, p. 17). The stronger relation `≼p` (Definition 7, p. 20) asks `p^{-1} R p ⊆ RA`; `Sp` satisfies it by construction.
4. Model-check the target formula on `Sp`, then use Theorem 2 (pp. 27–28) to transfer validity to `S` for the universal fragment `□Lμ`. Section 6.3 (pp. 29–30) also requires that `α` is consistent with the interpretation of every atomic proposition that occurs non-negated in the formula, which for `α = post[p]` is the check `pre[p](post[p](P)) = P` (p. 31); the paper says the method can be applied without consistency for propositions that occur only negated.

The paper's construction is explicit: “the abstract program must also be representable by some transition relation of the form `SA = (QA, RA)`” (Section 4, p. 18). For symbolic relations, represent `R(X,X')` and `p(X,Y)` as predicates, then eliminate concrete variables by existential quantification; the displayed construction is `R_i^p = ∃X∃X'. p(X,Y) ∧ p(X',Y') ∧ R_i(X,X')` (Section 4.2, pp. 20–21). The paper says the variables can be eliminated at least when the domains of the concrete and abstract variables are finite (p. 21).

Verus form. Inference: define `spec fn abs(s: Concrete) -> Abstract`, or a relation `spec fn relates(s,a) -> bool`; define an abstract transition predicate and prove a lemma that each concrete transition maps to an abstract transition. Use `requires`, `ensures`, `invariant` on loops, and `assert`/`assert forall` to discharge preservation. The paper's set transformers and existential elimination do not carry over as executable Verus code; they become quantified specifications and lemmas. Do not use `invariant(...)` as a call or `ensures` on a `spec fn`.

Evidence. The paper proves Proposition 11: if `p` is total, `S ⊑p Sp`; if additionally `p = p p^{-1} p`, `Sp` is faithful; if `p` is a (total) function, `Sp` is the least `p`-abstraction (p. 19). The concrete example abstracts an infinite readers/writers system to two booleans, `b1` meaning “there is no active reader” and `b2` meaning “there is no active writer” (Section 4.2, p. 21). The paper states: “As `p` is a total function, `RWp` is a faithful abstraction of `RW` via `p`.” (Section 4.2, p. 21). The resulting finite abstract system has five labeled transition relations (b-read, e-read, b-write, e-write, n-wait) and is shown in Figure 1 (p. 22). Mutual exclusion is then verified on it in “Reader/writer example continued” (Section 6.3, p. 31), using symbolic model checking; the abstract formula evaluates to true on `Sp`, and the paper concludes “By Theorem 2, we have that mutual exclusion holds on the concrete program.” (p. 31).

Limits and trust. The connection and simulation conditions are assumptions to be proved; the transfer theorem only covers its stated logic fragments and direction. The abstract system may add behaviors; if the check on it fails, the paper says one can “try to find a counter-example” or try “a more precise set of abstract states” (Section 6.3, p. 30), so a failed abstract check is not by itself a concrete counterexample. The paper also notes that reachability properties “do in general not hold on abstract systems” built by grouping states in this way (Section 6.3, p. 30). Inference: the BDD symbolic representation and model checker (described in the Conclusion, p. 36) would be trusted infrastructure; for agent-written verified Rust, the main threat is an incorrect abstraction relation or omitted transition case; Verus can prove the refinement implication but does not automatically validate that the chosen abstract property is the intended product requirement.

## technique 2: compositional abstraction

What it is. For parallel composition, derive a simulation of the composed system from component simulations. Section 7 defines three operators (Definition 10, p. 32): synchronous composition, asynchronous composition `|||`, and mixed composition `|[Δ]|` (the most general one). All allow shared variables. Theorem 5 treats synchronous composition, Theorem 6 asynchronous, and Theorem 7 mixed (pp. 34–36). The rule is (Comp): from `S1 ⊑p1 S1^A` and `S2 ⊑p2 S2^A` conclude `S1 op S2 ⊑p1∧p2 S1^A op S2^A` (Section 7.2, p. 34).

How to do it. Abstract each component with its own relation `p_i`, total on its variables and such that `p1 ∧ p2` is total on the combined variables (Assumption 1, p. 34); check the operator-specific hypothesis for part 1 of the theorem: the common-variable relations `p_ic` are functions (Theorems 5 and 7) or the common-variable relation is the same `p_c` for both components (Theorem 6); compose the abstract components; then apply the theorem to obtain a simulation of the concrete composition. Parts 2 and 3 compare the composition of component abstractions with the abstraction of the composed system, and need further hypotheses (functions or onto relations, see Theorems 5–7). Inference: in Verus, make each component transition lemma a reusable proof lemma and prove a composition lemma over the product state, preserving ownership or explicit disjointness invariants.

Evidence and failure. The theorems are algebraic results. The proofs of Theorems 5 and 6 are in Appendix A.3 (pp. 40–43); Theorem 7 is proved on p. 36 by expressing mixed composition through the other two (Lemma 4). The paper states that the required conditions on the `p_i` “are also necessary if no more information on the transition relations” is available (p. 40, proof of Theorem 5). Inference: synchronization mismatches and shared-state aliasing are the likely failure points in a Rust implementation.

## what is proved, measured, and trusted

The paper proves simulation and preservation theorems. It reports no benchmark numbers or runtimes. The Conclusion (p. 36) mentions a BDD-based symbolic verification tool, a protocol verified with it (where compositionality “was essential”), and a distributed cache memory case study in another paper, all without measurements. The readers/writers example demonstrates finite abstraction but gives no quantitative speedup. The paper says that choosing abstraction relations “requires a deep knowledge of the concrete program to be verified and cannot be automated” (p. 36). Inference: trusted elements include the transition-system semantics, the logical soundness of the μ-calculus/model checker, and correctness of any symbolic quantifier elimination. Inference: VL should reuse the relation-plus-preservation pattern, but should not redo the general Galois-connection theory; its engineering effort belongs in checking abstraction coverage and composing verified Rust components.

## techniques for the catalog

- galois-connection simulation — relate concrete and abstract state sets with `(α,γ)` and prove the predicate-transformer simulation inequality
- faithful relation abstraction — compute `Rp = p^{-1}Rp` for a total relation `p` (faithful when also `p = p p^{-1} p`) and verify the target property on the resulting abstract system
- symbolic abstraction — encode transitions and abstraction as predicates and eliminate concrete variables by existential quantification
- compositional simulation — derive a simulation of a parallel composition from component simulations under the operator-specific hypotheses
