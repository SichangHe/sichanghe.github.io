# A Storm is Coming: A Modern Probabilistic Model Checker

## scope and result
The paper presents Storm, an open-source probabilistic model checker. It analyzes discrete- and continuous-time Markov chains, Markov decision processes (MDPs), and Markov automata (MAs), using numerical or symbolic computation. The authors state: “It features the analysis of discrete- and continuous-time variants of both Markov chains and MDPs.” (abstract, p. 592). Storm supports Prism, JANI, generalized stochastic Petri nets, dynamic fault trees, pGCL probabilistic programs with observe-statements, and explicit-transition inputs (section 2, pp. 593–594). Its implementation is about 100,000 lines of C++ (introduction, p. 592).

A useful design lesson for verified systems is separation between model front ends, representations, engines, solver interfaces, and APIs. The paper says, “it has a modular set-up, enabling the easy exchange of different solvers and distinct decision diagram packages” (introduction, p. 593). Inference: a Verus product can expose proof-relevant interfaces between these layers, while keeping an untrusted parser or numerical backend behind explicit specifications.

## technique: choose a model and property
Inference: use DTMCs for discrete-time probabilistic transitions, CTMCs for continuous-time transitions, MDPs when actions introduce nondeterminism, and MAs when both probabilistic branching and exponential delays are needed. The paper defines the relationships: “CTMCs are MAs without non-determinism, while MDPs are MAs without delays; DTMCs are CTMCs without delays” (section 2, p. 593). Attach state rewards, and action rewards for nondeterministic models. Express branching-time reachability or reward properties in PCTL/CSL; conditional probabilities and rewards are supported for probabilistic programs (section 2, pp. 593–594).

Inference: an application workflow is: (1) select the stochastic model whose transitions and timing match the system; (2) encode it in Prism or JANI, or use a native GSPN, DFT, pGCL, or explicit-transition builder; (3) state a PCTL/CSL, reward, conditional, or long-run objective; (4) select an engine and solver; (5) run the query and inspect exactness and counterexamples where required. Inference: in Verus, represent finite-state transitions as Rust datatypes and prove invariants over `next` relations with `requires` and `ensures`; probabilistic distributions and real-valued rewards need a separately specified mathematical model because ordinary Verus integer proofs do not provide probability semantics.

## technique: select a representation and engine
Storm uses sparse matrices for fast operations on small or moderate models and MTBDDs for very large models. The paper explains: “sparse matrices allow for fast operations on small and moderately sized models, multi-terminal binary decision diagrams (MTBDDs) are able to represent gigantic models, however with slightly more expensive operations” (section 2, p. 594). The sparse engine builds a matrix directly; the exploration engine uses machine-learning-guided state-space exploration. The `dd` engine is purely decision-diagram based; `hybrid` combines MTBDDs and sparse matrices; abstraction-refinement turns possibly infinite discrete-time Markov models into finite stochastic games and refines them as necessary (section 2, p. 594).

Inference: an application workflow is: (1) estimate state-space size and choose sparse or MTBDD representation; (2) use exploration when discovering only relevant states is beneficial; (3) use hybrid when some operations are cheaper on sparse matrices; (4) use abstraction-refinement for infinite or very large discrete-time models; (5) compare engines on the same property. Inference: the analogous Verus choice is between executable finite maps/vectors and specification-level sets; prove that a compact representation preserves transition and invariant semantics before optimizing it.

## technique: exact arithmetic and counterexamples
Numerical model-checking methods can suffer rounding errors. Storm therefore has an exact-arithmetic mode (section 2, p. 594). For a refuted probabilistic property, it implements MILP-based counterexamples and MaxSAT-based high-level counterexamples for Prism models; high-level output can be Prism code (section 2, p. 594). Inference: an application workflow is: (1) run the ordinary query; (2) rerun with exact arithmetic when a threshold decision is sensitive; (3) if false, invoke MILP or MaxSAT generation; (4) inspect the resulting paths or Prism fragment as a concrete explanation. Inference: a Verus counterpart is a proof-producing witness: define a counterexample datatype, prove that each witness path satisfies transition constraints and violates the property, and keep the solver-generated witness outside the trusted kernel until checked.

## technique: solver abstraction
Storm places a solver interface beneath engines. It offers sparse and MTBDD linear/Bellman equation solvers, stochastic-game solvers, MILP, and SMT back ends. “As the performance characteristics of different backend solvers can vary drastically for the same input, this permits choosing the best solver for a given task” (section 3, p. 596). Inference: an application workflow is: (1) express a query as the interface’s equation, game, MILP, or SMT problem; (2) select a backend compatible with the representation and license; (3) solve; (4) keep the engine independent of backend details. Inference: Verus can specify a solver result with a postcondition and verify a checker for that result; the external optimizer itself remains trusted unless its certificate is checked.

## evidence, limits, and trust
The evaluation used an HP BL685C G7, up to eight 2.0-GHz cores, 8 GB memory, and a 1,800-second timeout (section 4, p. 596). It compared Storm and Prism on 24 models and 82 properties from the Prism benchmark suite, with multiple instances per model. Storm solved 361 of 380 tasks, while Prism solved 346 (discussion, p. 598); the denominator is all 380 tasks, not just completed tasks. Storm's exact mode was reported up to three orders of magnitude faster than Prism's exact mode (p. 598), and Storm was compared with IMCA on MA models (p. 598). These are performance measurements, not proofs that every model or solver result is correct. Inference: the trusted base includes parsers, state-space builders, numerical or symbolic libraries, and backend solvers; exact arithmetic reduces numerical risk but does not verify modeling assumptions. Limitations include no fully symbolic continuous-time engine, no equivalent of Prism’s hybrid engine, no LTL model checking, no statistical model checking, no probabilistic timed automata, and no stochastic-game input support (introduction, p. 593; section 3, p. 595). Inference: the threat to an agent-written verified-Rust product is moderate when the product relies on external solver answers or floating-point probability thresholds; it is lower when a small Verus checker validates certificates and model invariants.

## what VL should reuse
Storm supplies front-end independence, multiple representations, interchangeable solvers, exact arithmetic, and counterexample generation. Inference: these are useful patterns for verified systems; the paper does not supply a verified counterexample checker. Inference: Inference: VL should avoid rebuilding a general probabilistic model checker; it should reuse the separation and focus on verified Rust kernels and certificate checkers. The paper does not measure Verus, Rust, or proof effort, so portability claims are inferences.

## techniques for the catalog
- model-aligned probabilistic checking: choose DTMC, CTMC, MDP, or MA and state PCTL/CSL or reward objectives
- representation and engine selection: switch between sparse, MTBDD, hybrid, exploration, and abstraction-refinement engines
- exact and witness-producing analysis: rerun sensitive queries with exact arithmetic and generate MILP/MaxSAT counterexamples
- solver abstraction: hide interchangeable equation, game, MILP, and SMT back ends behind one interface
