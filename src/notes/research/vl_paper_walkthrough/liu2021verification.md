# Verification and Redesign of OFDM Backscatter
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `liu2021verification`, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi21/presentation/liu-xin)

The paper experimentally diagnoses failures of existing symbol-level OFDM backscatter with phase error correction enabled and redesigns it as TScatter. Cyclic-prefix removal discards phase transitions within the prefix; pilot-based phase error correction can then erase a symbol-wide phase offset. The abstract states: “the phase error correction in WiFi receivers can eliminate the phase offset created by a tag” (Abstract, p. 939). TScatter moves modulation to individual samples, uses coding that survives phase correction, and builds a receiver model that estimates the tag data.

## technique 1: verify the failure mechanism before redesign

Inference: Use this when a physical-layer design relies on a receiver implementation detail. The authors rebuilt prior systems using USRPs running an 802.11g stack with phase error correction and tested packet-level and symbol-level synchronization. Figure 2 reports 128,000 transmitted data points (Section 1, p. 940). In a separate USRP B210 experiment, two wires provide common ground and a starting signal; varying the delay after that signal places the inversion point at different positions within the 4 µs symbol (Section 3.2, p. 942). When a transition falls inside the cyclic prefix, prefix removal discards it and phase correction eliminates the remaining symbol-wide phase offset. When transitions fall inside useful symbols, opposite offsets in successive symbols become almost the same after correction, so the old XOR decoder produces the same result for different tag bits (Sections 3.2–3.3, p. 942). The paper reports around 50% BER even at high SNR for the packet-level experiment (Figure 2, p. 940) and says: “when the inversion points are within the cyclic prefix zone (i.e., the inversion point delay is less than 0.7 µs), the decoded tag data are all zeros” (Section 3.2, p. 942).

The analysis identifies two requirements: the inversion point must fall in the useful symbol, and tag data must use phase offsets that correction cannot eliminate (Section 3.4, p. 942). Inference: This turns an empirical failure into a design constraint. Inference: a Verus model could represent samples, CP removal, and phase correction as pure `spec fn`s over sequences, then prove that a chosen encoding preserves distinguishability. The hardware timing and radio channel remain assumptions; a Verus proof of the discrete model cannot establish RF behavior.

## technique 2: sample-level modulation with orthogonal coding

Inference: Use sample-level modulation when symbol-level phase changes are erased by receiver correction and precise CP synchronization is too expensive for a low-power tag. An 802.11g symbol has 64 useful IDFT samples plus 16 copied CP samples. Toggle the RF switch at 20 MHz so each 0.05 µs switch cycle matches one sample. Multiply each sample by a square-wave harmonic, selecting phase θn from the tag data; the two sidebands shift to ±20 MHz and avoid the original channel (Section 4, p. 943). The paper gives the design statement: “Our sample-level modulation has two design goals: i) embedding the tag data in the OFDM signal at the sample-level, and ii) minimizing the interference from the original band channel” (Section 4, p. 943).

For low BER, divide tag data into groups and map each to a predefined nearly orthogonal 40-bit pseudo-random-noise sequence. Transmit each sequence twice so one copy remains after CP removal. The receiver chooses the best matching sequence rather than estimating every bit (Section 5.2, p. 944). For high throughput, combine a rate-2/3 convolutional code with predefined samples; at 64QAM use 32 coded samples and 8 predefined samples in each 40-sample sequence, and at 16QAM use 18 coded and 22 predefined samples (Section 5.3, p. 944).

Inference: in Verus, model the PN lookup table as a ghost map and prove pairwise correlation or a bounded separation predicate. State `requires` for a valid sequence index and `ensures` for decoded-index equality under a noise bound. A `proof fn` can prove that duplicating the 40-sample sequence leaves a copy after removing 16 CP samples. The analog switch, Fourier transform, and noise bound require separately validated contracts.

## technique 3: model the receiver and decode by minimum distance

The receiver pipeline is: detect the two long-training-sequence correlation peaks, synchronize, correct carrier-frequency offset (CFO), remove the cyclic prefix, perform FFT, track pilots and phase-correct subcarriers, then decode the tag sequence (Figure 11 and Section 6.2, p. 945; Appendix B, pp. 952–953). After CFO correction, the model is `R_n = e^{j(β+φ)}e^{jθ_n}S_n` (Eq. 11, Section 6.1, p. 944; Eq. 22, Appendix B, p. 953), where β models envelope detection delay, φ channel phase, and θ_n tag modulation. Continuous square waves reflect the preamble without embedding tag data. Under that condition, Appendix B derives that the common phase offset does not change the positions of the two correlation peaks (Eq. 19, p. 952). Phase error correction removes β + φ while leaving a tag-dependent correction that the decoder models (Section 6.2.2, p. 945). The decoder reconstructs original samples using Eq. 8 and minimizes squared Euclidean distance between the predicted corrected data subcarriers and their receiver-reported nearest QAM points, rather than applying the old XOR decoder (Section 6.3, pp. 945–946). A sliding-window search finds the best header alignment; a predefined trailer marks the end (Section 5.1, p. 944).

Inference: encode candidate sequences and the Euclidean-distance decoder as executable Rust over fixed-size arrays, with `ensures` that the returned candidate is minimal under a uniqueness assumption. Loop `invariant` clauses can preserve bounds and accumulated distances. Verus can prove arithmetic identities in the discrete model, but not that a physical FFT implementation, ADC, or channel meets them without a hardware abstraction.

## evidence and limits

Experiments used a ThinkPad T420s transmitter and receivers implemented using a DELL XPS 9550 laptop and USRP-B210 with an 802.11g PHY. Receiver 1 obtains original WiFi information; receiver 2 listens for the backscattered signal. The transmitter-to-tag distance was 1 meter, and 64QAM was the default WiFi modulation. Tests covered a library, hallway, and stadium (Section 8.1, pp. 946–947). The paper defines throughput as “correctly demodulated data bits at PHY layer” and BER as “the number of bit errors divided by the total number of transmitted bits” (Section 8.1, p. 947). The comparison uses prior systems’ reported best results (Section 8.2, p. 947). At similar throughput, TScatter's BER is three to four orders of magnitude lower than MOXcatter; at similar BER it reports more than 212 times MOXcatter's throughput (Abstract, p. 939). In the library, at 120 feet average BER remained around 10^-3 for Nibble and 10^-4 for Binary (Section 8.3, p. 947). The paper reports 10.61 Mbps for 4-phase modulation (Section 8.2, p. 947). Section 9 reports around 5–10 Mbps PHY throughput and calculates a 288 Kbps requirement for generated 12-channel EKG at 1 KHz and 24-bit resolution. The sufficiency claim includes expected body attenuation and upper-layer overhead: “TScatter’s throughput should be sufficient for 12-channel EKG measurements” (Section 9, p. 950).

The full design was tested using USRPs. Commodity deployment remains prospective: “to implement the full version of TScatter on commodity devices, we expect to address the following engineering problems” (Section 11, p. 950). These are obtaining bad-checksum packets using monitor mode and learning the scrambling seed. Inference: The paper’s verification combines experimental diagnosis and mathematical modeling; it does not provide a machine-checked proof of radio or receiver behavior.

## what is proved, measured, and trusted

The paper derives equations for modulation, phase correction, carrier-frequency and sampling-frequency offset correction, and synchronization, and measures BER and throughput over tested distances. Power is estimated by circuit simulation, rather than measured on the FPGA prototype: “We designed an integrated circuit for TScatter’s digital processing module and conducted a simulation using Cadence Spectre for TSMC 65nm process” (Section 8.8, p. 949). The reported total is 30.2 µW. Inference: A proof based on the sampled equations would still trust their correspondence to RF behavior, the receiver implementation, channel assumptions, PN lookup table, and numerical decoder; this paper supplies no end-to-end machine-checked proof.

## techniques for the catalog

- receiver-aware failure analysis — test prior encodings against cyclic-prefix removal and pilot phase correction before redesigning
- sample-level OFDM modulation — toggle at one sample per cycle and shift sidebands away from the original channel
- duplicated orthogonal PN coding — map data to nearly orthogonal sequences, transmit twice, and decode by best match
- model-based minimum-distance decoding — derive the receiver's correction equations and choose the candidate with least Euclidean distance
