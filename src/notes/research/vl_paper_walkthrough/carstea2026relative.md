# Relative formalization in Isabelle/HOL of a result in inverse problems
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `carstea2026relative`, [paper](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23659v1)

## purpose and result
This paper reports a proof-of-concept formalization of an inverse-problems theorem about piecewise-polynomial anisotropic conductivities (the author's preprint arXiv:2606.15977) in Isabelle/HOL. It translates the main theorem, definitions, and cited results, then proves the target relative to explicitly declared literature assumptions. The abstract states: “The proof of the result is relative to a number of external results which were deemed to be well known and generally accepted to be true.” (Abstract, p. 1) The formalization was produced with Codex and ChatGPT 5.6 Sol on a $200/month subscription, in under a week, which the author puts at about $50 (§1, p. 1). The Isabelle code for the theorem, definitions, and imported results is about 50 KB or 1,400 lines (§3, p. 2). The paper itself prints the code only as definitions, statements, and locales in Appendices A and B (pp. 5–154, two independent back-translations of the same material), plus one proved theorem (`cgu_graph_boundary_regularities_imply_lipschitz_domain`, pp. 14–15); the lemma proofs are in the author's GitHub repository (§2, p. 2), which this study did not read.

## technique 1: relative formalization with explicit interfaces
Use this when a theorem depends on substantial published mathematics that is not being reproved. Translate the main theorem with its hypotheses and relevant definitions into Isabelle/HOL; translate each cited result into an Isabelle `locale`; assume those results rather than prove them; reconstruct the target proof as machine-checked lemmas (§2, p. 2). The author's reason for assuming: “Without this measure the formalization work would have probably taken considerably more time.” (§2, p. 2) The final theorem records that the conclusion follows from Isabelle’s foundations and libraries plus the identified literature base. Completion means Isabelle checks the exact intended theorem-language version end to end, with no omitted proofs or provisional assumptions, and every dependency is either a checked library, a proved project component, or a declared literature interface (§2, p. 2).

In the appendices the literature base is 12 atomic locales, each assuming one “claim” definition, cited to Hörmander, Evans, Kang–Yun, Lassas–Taylor–Uhlmann, and McLean (Appendix A, pp. 47–55; statements pp. 58–76). Ten are labelled “Relationship to source: derived” and two “direct” (`hormander_regular_distribution_injectivity_claim`, `mclean_graph_supported_h1_zero_claim`). So most assumed results are the author's adaptations of book or paper results, not verbatim theorems. For the Kang–Yun item the paper says: “Neither paper prints this exact graph-domain implication verbatim, and LTU supplies corroboration only.” (Appendix A, p. 65)

Inference: In Verus, represent imported mathematics as explicit `assume`d facts or `external_body` boundaries only where the project’s trusted-code policy permits them, and document the assumption in the contract or a separate trusted interface. Do not mark the function under verification as `external_body`; that would remove the proof obligation. A proved Rust theorem can therefore be relative to assumptions, but the assumptions must be visible in the dependency record. The "derived" labels above suggest also recording, for each assumption, whether it is verbatim from the source or adapted.

## technique 2: long-running harness with fixed project context
The author treats formalization as one extended project. Codex reads the mathematical source, searches Isabelle libraries, writes and checks proofs, and uses a project-specific harness containing reusable instructions, operation rules, tools, templates, and mathematical context (§2, p. 2). The harness includes “instructions about what information was to be treated as fixed and when a question should be left for human review”, allowing long tasks with little supervision while leaving the Isabelle files and a record of the intermediate work for later inspection (§2, p. 2).

## technique 3: translation testing and freezing
Translation is the main weakness (§3, p. 2). The paper lists four translation tests (§3, pp. 2–3):
- all translations were checked and approved by ChatGPT 5.6 Sol Pro (in the chat, outside the Codex context where they were produced) and by Claude Opus 5;
- the main-theorem translation was done first and kept fixed throughout, and translations of cited results were frozen after being made; the stated purpose is to prevent the LLM from “independently altering the statement until it got something that would allow it to more quickly finish its task”;
- ChatGPT 5.6 Sol in Codex (effort: extra high) was asked to try for 24 hours to derive a contradiction between the translated literature statements and the Isabelle libraries; “It found none.”;
- Codex back-translated the Isabelle definitions and statements of the main theorem and imported results into English using subagents without access to the original manuscripts, done twice independently. The results are Appendices A and B: interlinear Isabelle/English for definitions, and English original, Isabelle code, and back-translation for theorem statements.

The paper records one real correction: the version of the paper given to Codex “was imprecise regarding what is meant by a ‘Lipschitz domain’, and the initial translation took it in a sense that was different from what one of the cited results used.” (§3, p. 3) The author also notes that a small error in translating the main theorem may only misstate the English theorem slightly, whereas “a small mistake in translating a known result from a monograph, on the other hand, could in principle invalidate the whole project” (§3, p. 3).

Inference: For Verus, freeze the Rust specification before proof search, run boundary-value and invariant counterexample searches, and ask an agent without source access to paraphrase the contract, then compare the paraphrase with the original requirement. Agreement between paraphrases is evidence about consistency, not semantic proof. The paper does not say who compared the back-translations with the originals, or how.

## technique 4: definition-first, readable interface
The appendices expose definitions such as polynomial evaluation (`cgu_poly_eval`, p. 6), Lipschitz domains (pp. 11–13), weak gradients (`cgu_weak_gradient_on`, pp. 26–27), trace norms (`mclean_trace_norm`, p. 35), and PDE solutions (`cgu_weak_solution_on`, p. 30), each with an English “mathematical role” and a back-translation. For example, `cgu_lipschitz_domain` is described as a “bi-Lipschitz half-space domain” (p. 11) and `cgu_graph_lipschitz_domain_v5` as a “Lipschitz-graph domain” built on it (pp. 12–13). The main theorem `cgu_global_uniqueness_claim_v7` (MAIN-001, p. 56) assumes n ≥ 3 and concludes `P1 = P2`, which the back-translation says is “equality of the entire coefficient families as finite coefficient representations, not merely almost-everywhere or pointwise equality of their evaluated matrices” (p. 57). The back-translations also flag totalization points, for example for `mclean_trace_norm`: “The definition does not separately assert that this set is nonempty or bounded below.” (p. 35)

Inference: In Verus, define small `spec fn`s and predicates for state invariants, then prove bridge lemmas before the top-level postcondition. Keep definitions total and expose assumptions such as positivity, boundedness, or differentiability explicitly in `requires`; do not hide them in executable code. The paper does not describe a lemma-by-lemma proof order, so "definition-first" here refers to how the statement and definitions are laid out for review.

## evidence, trust, and limits
The formalization checks Isabelle syntax and proof obligations, but it does not prove the external literature results. The author names two weaknesses: “the possibility of bugs in the Isabelle kernel” and translation (§3, p. 2). He states: “In a strict mathematical sense, without perfect confidence in the correspondence between natural language mathematical statements and their corresponding Isabelle code translations this experiment proves little.” (§4, p. 3) His “personal impression” is that, together with enough varied translation tests, a formalization that checks “does raise the level of confidence in the result somewhat”, and that the experiment “does show that relative formalization in Isabelle/HOL is achievable at least in some cases” (§4, pp. 3–4). He calls the work “a proof of concept” (§1, p. 1). Inference: this is not evidence that all research papers can be formalized cheaply, since it is one paper by one author. Inference: for agent-written verified Rust, relative formalization reduces proof burden while preserving a large trust surface: imported assumptions, translation errors, and library axioms can invalidate the product claim even when Verus verifies every local lemma.

## techniques for the catalog
- relative theorem interfaces — encode cited results as explicit locale-like assumptions and prove the target relative to them
- persistent formalization harness — preserve instructions, versions, templates, and review questions across long agent runs
- translation freeze and audit — freeze statements, search for contradictions, and independently back-translate code
- definition-first reconstruction — formalize typed definitions and bridge lemmas before the main theorem
