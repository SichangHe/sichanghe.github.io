# Cheetah: Lean and Fast Secure Two-Party Deep Neural Network Inference
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `huang2022cheetah`, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/huang-zhicong)

## what is formally established
Cheetah is a system for private two-party neural-network inference. Alice holds the model and Bob holds the input. The paper states: “Cheetah is designed for the two-party semi-honest setting in which both of parties follow the specification of the protocol and only one of the them is corrupted by an adversary.” (Section 2.5.3, p. 813) Appendix A specifies a static adversary and simulation-based security. Bob learns the architecture and inference result; Alice may learn the result or nothing, depending on the application. Model parameters and Bob's input otherwise remain private. Attacks based solely on inference results are outside this protection (Section 2.5.3, pp. 813–814).

Theorems 1–4 state realization of ideal functionalities for HomFC, basic HomCONV, HomBN, and full HomCONV. Appendix D proves convolution correctness and semi-honest privacy, then says the other theorem proofs follow similarly. Section 4 describes comparison and approximate truncation using OT; these should not be confused with the exact truncation functionality in Figure 7b. The authors state: “The layers of modern DNNs consist of alternating linear and non-linear operations.” (Section 1.1, p. 810)

## technique 1: encode linear layers as polynomial multiplication

Inference: Use this when a convolution, batch-normalization, or fully connected layer can be expressed as many inner products. Choose a plaintext ring `A_N,p = Z_p[X]/(X^N+1)`. Define input and weight encodings `π_i^F` and `π_w^F` so each desired inner product lands in a known coefficient. This removes SIMD rotations and permits additive shares from `Z_{2^ℓ}`. For HomFC, partition vectors and matrices into blocks with `n_i^w n_o^w ≤ N`, zero-pad, and encode each block.

The HomFC protocol's steps (Figure 2, p. 814) are: Bob partitions and encodes his input share, encrypts each polynomial under RLWE, and sends ciphertexts; Alice partitions and encodes her share and weights; Alice adds her encoded input share to each ciphertext, multiplies by the encoded weight blocks, and sums across input blocks; Alice extracts only output coefficients and re-masks each LWE ciphertext by subtracting a random `r[i]`; Alice sends the re-masked ciphertexts and outputs `r+b mod p`; Bob decrypts the already re-masked ciphertexts to obtain his output share, with no further subtraction. Extract is essential because other polynomial coefficients contain information beyond `Wv`.

Theorem 1 states that HomFC realizes the fully connected ideal functionality over `Z_p` against a semi-honest admissible adversary (Section 3.1, p. 815). It also gives Proposition 1 that coefficient `i·n_i+n_i−1` equals output `u[i]`, which is the correctness bridge from polynomial arithmetic to matrix multiplication.

Inference: In Verus, model `π_i`, `π_w`, and coefficient extraction as `spec fn`s over mathematical sequences, then prove the coefficient equation with `proof fn` lemmas. Executable Rust can call a verified polynomial-kernel API with `requires` for dimensions and modulus; `ensures` should state output shares sum to the ideal matrix product. Cryptographic encryption, randomness, and constant-time primitives remain external specifications unless verified separately.

## technique 2: convolution and batch normalization encodings

For convolution, encode tensor `T` at coefficient `cHW+iW+j`; encode kernel `K` in reverse offsets so output position `(c',i',j')` lands at `O−c'CHW+i'sW+j's`, where `O=HW(MC−1)+W(h−1)+h−1` (Figure 4, p. 815). The basic HomCONV sequence is Bob encoding/encrypting his share; Alice encoding her share and kernel; adding Alice's input polynomial to Bob's ciphertext and multiplying the sum by the kernel polynomial; extracting and re-masking each output coefficient; keeping the mask as her output share and sending only the re-masked ciphertexts for Bob to decrypt. For large tensors, partition filters and channels without overlap and sum the channel contributions. Spatial blocks overlap by `h−1` rows or columns when `h>1`, so convolution windows survive the partition; zero-pad boundary blocks (Section 3.2.1, p. 816; Appendix C, pp. 824–825).

For BN, rewrite `T'[c,i,j]=µ[c]T[c,i,j]+θ[c]` as channel-wise scalar-vector products and additions (Section 2.5, p. 813). HomBN stacks channels into one polynomial when `C²HW≤N`; otherwise it partitions into blocks satisfying `C_w²H_wW_w≤N`. Bob encrypts his encoded blocks; Alice adds her encoded share, multiplies by encoded scaling coefficients, extracts the required outputs, subtracts a random mask, and sends ciphertexts. Alice keeps the mask plus `θ`; Bob decrypts (Figure 6 and Section 3.3, pp. 816–817). Theorem 2 states that HomCONV realizes `FCONV` for `MCHW≤N` in the semi-honest model (Section 3.2, p. 815); Theorem 3 gives the analogous HomBN realization (Section 3.3, p. 817).

Inference: A Verus proof should separate shape arithmetic from cryptographic correctness. Use `requires` for `MCHW≤N`, stride and alignment bounds, and `ensures` that every output index equals the specified sum. A loop `invariant` can establish that processed blocks preserve all earlier output coefficients. The paper's theorem assumes exact ring arithmetic and idealized cryptographic primitives; it does not verify a Rust implementation.

## technique 3: approximate truncation with an explicit error budget

Fixed-point multiplication doubles precision, so truncate low `f` bits. Local truncation has a mild one-bit error `e0` with probability 1/2 and a harsh error `e1` with probability `x/2^ℓ`. Cheetah corrects the wrap-around error while retaining a last-bit error: its output is either `x >> f` or `(x >> f)−1` (Figure 8, p. 818). The experiment in Section 6.5 reports identical SqueezeNet classification labels to SCI HE on about 1,000 ImageNet images; this is narrower evidence than an accuracy guarantee for arbitrary models. Its VOLE-based one-bit approximate protocol costs `13ℓ` communication bits; when the most significant bit is known, one 1-out-of-2 OT computes Boolean shares of the wrap-around bit, followed by Boolean-to-arithmetic conversion and local correction; the whole truncation costs `f+4` bits (Table 4, p. 819). Known MSB is common immediately after ReLU, where it is zero.

Inference: In Verus, represent the truncation result as an exact value plus a bounded error ghost variable. Prove an `ensures` bound such as `|error|≤1` for the one-bit protocol, and make the neural-network specification state whether that error is acceptable. A proof cannot infer statistical model accuracy; that requires the paper's empirical test or a separate numerical theorem.

## technique 4: composition and optimization evidence

Section 5.1 reports that BN fusion saves a HomBN invocation and one truncation (p. 819). Inference: its layer order needs clarification before implementation: the section first describes BN after CONV, but then says “for each BN-then-CONV structure in the DNN”. The claimed kernel scaling and subsequent addition of `θ` require a correctness proof for the chosen order.

Section 5.2 proposes sending one copy of a shared ciphertext vector component and omitting low ciphertext bits (p. 819). The authors report a decryption failure probability below `2^-38` for the latter optimization and a saving of 16–25% of the communication sent by Alice, rather than of total protocol communication.

The formal claims rely on semi-honest security reductions; the implementation uses SEAL/HEXL and EMP with `N=4096`, `q≈2^105`, `p=2^37`, `σ=3.2`, and claims at least 128-bit security according to SEAL (Section 6.1, p. 819). The abstract claims about 5.6× lower ResNet50 WAN time and 12.9× lower communication than CrypTFlow2 (p. 809). Table 8 reports Cheetah WAN times/communication of 39.1 s/0.5 GB for SqueezeNet, 134.7 s/2.3 GB for ResNet50, and 177.7 s/2.4 GB for DenseNet121. These runs use four threads and fixed-point precision `f=12`, a 2.70 GHz processor and 16 GB RAM; WAN bandwidth is about 44 MBps with 40 ms round-trip time. Timings include communication but exclude HE key generation and base OT; communication excludes the one-time setup (Sections 6.1 and 6.4, pp. 819–821). The three-party SecureQ8 comparison reports faster LAN execution for SecureQ8 and faster WAN execution for Cheetah, which the authors attribute to less communication (Section 6.4, p. 821).

Inference: For VeruLaw, the reusable methods are ideal-functionality contracts, coefficient-level correctness lemmas, explicit error specifications, and composition proofs. The threat to agent-written verified Rust is high for cryptographic code: a proved protocol contract can still be instantiated with wrong parameters, a non-constant-time primitive, a faulty randomness source, or a malicious-party behavior outside the semi-honest model. Existing Cheetah already covers these protocol designs; VL should not reimplement them without a concrete Rust/Verus correctness target.

## techniques for the catalog

- polynomial encoding: map each fitting input/weight block to coefficients so polynomial multiplication computes its linear-layer outputs
- extract-and-remask: reveal only required coefficients and hide them with one-time random masks
- ideal-functionality proof: state protocol inputs/outputs and prove realization under semi-honest adversaries
- error-budget truncation: remove harsh fixed-point errors while explicitly retaining and measuring a one-bit error
- known-MSB truncation: replace comparison with one OT when the input sign is known
