# Scaling Generative Verifiers For Natural Language Mathematical Proof Verification And Selection
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `mahdavi2025scaling`, [paper](http://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13027v1)

## what is proved or measured
The paper evaluates two generative proof-verification methods: LLM-as-a-Judge, which scores one proof, and GenSelect, which compares candidate proofs. It uses VerProofArena (204 proofs with binary human labels), VerProofBench (429 proofs scored by expert graders on a 7-point rubric, binarized at score 6), SelProofBench (29 problems), SelOPC, and final-answer sets including Challenge-19. The authors study prompt choice, reinforcement learning (RL), step-based judgment, and test-time selection at large token budgets. The abstract reports, “We further show that the choice of prompt for LLM-as-a-Judge significantly affects the model’s performance, but reinforcement learning can reduce this sensitivity.”

The paper’s central warning is that RL improves proof-level judge metrics without improving final-answer precision, and that a strong judge can still accept invalid proofs. In its hard-problem check, GPT-5 judged 7 of 48 generated candidates correct even though all seven had critical mathematical errors; its accuracy was 41/48 = 87.2% in that setting (§9, p. 9). This is a denominator and model-specific result, not a general GPT-5 error rate.

## technique: LLM-as-a-Judge with prompt variants
What it is: Prompt a model to judge a complete proof, either with a binary label or a 7-point quality score; sample several independent judgments and aggregate them by majority or average.
When to use it: Use for ranking or filtering candidate proofs when a human-labeled benchmark or another correctness signal is available for calibration.
How to do it (paper’s §3, p. 2, and §5, p. 4): (1) Choose a prompt: General Summary, OPC, or GIMO. (2) Change only the output formatting to standardized XML tags. (3) Ask the model to judge the whole proof. (4) Sample `n_j` judgments per proof and aggregate. (5) For proof selection, choose the proof with the highest score. The paper states, “In the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm [30, 34, 26], a language model directly evaluates the correctness of a single proof, typically producing either a binary judgement or a fine-grained score (e.g., on a 7-point scale).”
Evidence and limits: On the VerProofArena evaluation with Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 in Figure 1 (right), the prompt changes the tradeoff: General Summary has proof precision 18.28 and recall 99.89; GIMO has 57.58 precision and 87.06 recall; OPC has 22.88 precision and 99.96 recall (majority@5 results; Table 5 gives the full benchmark context). The paper says “OPC strikes a balance between the two” and adopts OPC as its default (§5, p. 4). These percentages are proof-judgment metrics for the stated model and dataset, not correctness of generated mathematics. Inference: prompt choice must be treated as a measured hyperparameter, not a cosmetic detail.
Verus form: Inference: use an LLM judge only to prioritize candidate Rust proofs or identify likely missing obligations. The final `proof fn` and `ensures` remain checked by Verus; a judge score cannot replace a verified postcondition.

## technique: RL calibration of a proof judge
What it is: Fine-tune a judge with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a binary reward for matching ground-truth judgments.
When to use it: Use when a labeled proof-judgment training set exists and prompt variation causes unstable precision/recall.
How to do it (paper’s §5, pp. 4–5, and §8, p. 8): (1) Start with the OPC training set. (2) Reserve 277 proofs as a validation set. (3) Train Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 (and separately Qwen3-8B) with DAPO/GRPO on the reward “judgement matches ground truth.” (4) Compare training curves under different prompts. (5) Evaluate on in-distribution and out-of-distribution proof sets. Training used batch size 1024 (64 prompts × 16 rollouts), maximum generation length 16,384, and 128 H100 GPUs (§8, p. 8).
Evidence and limits: The authors report that RL significantly improves proof-judgment performance and closes the initial OPC/GIMO gap (Figure 1, §5). On the VerProofBench table for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507, OPC RL has precision 73.66, recall 66.15, F1 69.68; OPC RL (Rubric) has 77.21, 75.85, 76.51 (Table 6, 429 proof denominator). However, RL does not materially improve final-answer precision: the paper says the roughly 2% change is within standard deviation (§5, p. 5). The authors hypothesize that the proof-level gain comes from identifying “incorrect style of proofs such as missing justifications” and better calibration, not from better reasoning about the mathematical content (Takeaway 3). Inference: RL reward design can optimize judge behavior without making the judge semantically reliable.
Verus form: Inference: RL can propose proof obligations or rank failed Verus attempts, but only Verus verification establishes a Rust theorem. Keep any learned judge outside the trusted computing base.

## technique: whole-proof judgment versus step-based judgment
What it is: Whole-proof judgment evaluates the proof at once. Step-based judgment first inserts step separators, judges each highlighted step, and marks the proof correct only if all steps pass.
When to use it: Inference: use step-based judgment only when false negatives are acceptable; otherwise prefer whole-proof judgment, which the paper found better.
How to do it (paper’s §5, p. 6): (1) Ask the model to decompose the proof into steps. (2) Reprompt with each step highlighted. (3) Ask whether each step is correct. (4) Aggregate with an all-steps-correct rule. The paper reports, “We observe higher precision, but significantly lower recall and accuracy.”
Evidence and limits: Table 3 (Appendix A, p. 14) compares vanilla and step-based judgment on VerProofBench with GPT-OSS-120B (majority@5, averaged over 32 seeds): precision 73.62 vs 89.43, recall 78.51 vs 36.36, F1 75.97 vs 51.69, correct judgements 89.12 vs 85.11. The paper says the strict all-steps-correct requirement “likely leads to many false negatives” and concludes whole-proof judgment is best among tested strategies (Takeaway 4). Inference: requiring every generated obligation to pass resembles a conjunction of Verus proof obligations, but an LLM’s step labels are not proof certificates.

## technique: GenSelect tournaments
What it is: Compare proofs directly instead of scoring each in isolation. Pairwise GenSelect compares all pairs and selects the proof with the most wins; knockout GenSelect uses single elimination.
When to use it: Use pairwise comparison when accuracy matters and the candidate set is small; use knockout when hundreds of candidates make quadratic comparisons too expensive.
How to do it (paper’s §3, pp. 2–3, and §6, p. 6): (1) Generate `n_p` candidate proofs by temperature sampling. (2) For pairwise GenSelect, compare every pair and select the highest-win proof. (3) For knockout, compare candidates in single-elimination rounds, requiring exactly `n_p − 1` comparisons. (4) Repeat seeds because the paper observes high variance. The paper states, “A knockout tournament operates in single-elimination format, requiring exactly `n_p − 1` comparisons to select a winner from `n_p` candidates.”
Evidence and limits: On SelProofBench and SelOPC with GPT-OSS-120B and Qwen3-235B-A22-Thinking-2507, there is no clear winner between pairwise GenSelect and LLM-as-a-Judge; the best method varies by dataset and model (§6, p. 6, Takeaway 5). A 3.44% accuracy difference there equals one problem because 29 × 0.0344 ≈ 1, a denominator explicitly noted by the authors. Inference: small selection sets make apparent percentage differences unstable.
Verus form: Inference: use pairwise comparison to choose among candidate verified Rust patches only after each candidate compiles and passes Verus; selecting a proof that was never checked is unsafe.

## technique: hybrid knockout plus LLM-as-a-Judge selection
What it is: Use cheap knockout GenSelect to reduce hundreds of proofs to `n_s`, then sample `n_j` independent LLM-as-a-Judge scores for each survivor and select the highest average.
When to use it: Use when candidate generation is wide but full pairwise comparison or judging every candidate is too expensive.
How to do it (paper’s §7, pp. 7–8): (1) Generate `n_p` proofs. (2) Run knockout GenSelect to retain `n_s`. (3) Sample `n_j` judgments for each retained proof. (4) Average correctness scores and return the highest. `n_s = 1, n_j = 0` is pure knockout; `n_s = n_p` is pure LLM-as-a-Judge. The paper says, “The hybrid combination of LLM-as-a-Judge with GenSelect performs substantially better than GenSelect alone, or majority voting.”
Evidence and limits: In Challenge-19, GPT-OSS-120B averaged over eight seeds achieved 96.05% with `n_p=256, n_s=16, n_j=32`, at ×1008 LLM calls per problem; knockout with 256 candidates achieved 92.10% at ×511, majority@512 82.23% at ×512, and pass@1 47.07% at ×1 (Figure 3, p. 8). On AIME-2025, the hybrid (`n_p=128, n_s=16, n_j=32`) reached 100% over eight seeds at ×752 calls; knockout alone (`n_p=128`) got 97.5% at ×255, LLM-as-a-Judge (`n_p=128, n_j=32`) 100% at ×4224, pass@1 was 91.26% and majority@128 97.08%. Other hybrid settings on Challenge-19 were lower: 91.44% at `n_p=128` (×752) and 94.07% at `n_p=512` (×1520), so 96.05% is the best configuration, not typical. The paper’s preliminary USAMO-2025 problem-2 experiment selected a correct proof 50% of the time versus zero for no-test-time-scaling GenSelect, but this is one problem and not a benchmark-wide rate (§7, p. 8). Inference: hybrid selection trades a large, explicit call budget for accuracy and remains dependent on judge reliability.
Verus form: Inference: apply this only to candidates already independently verified by Verus, or use it to prioritize verification order. The hybrid’s LLM calls cannot establish Rust safety.

## techniques for the catalog
- LLM-as-a-Judge — score complete proofs with binary or 7-point prompts and aggregate sampled judgments
- GRPO/DAPO judge calibration — train a judge on ground-truth matching rewards and test prompt sensitivity
- step-based judgment — split a proof into steps and aggregate per-step labels, with a recall cost
- GenSelect tournaments — choose proofs by pairwise wins using quadratic or knockout comparisons
- hybrid proof selection — prefilter with knockout GenSelect, then score survivors with repeated judges
