# CLN2INV: Learning Loop Invariants with Continuous Logic Networks
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `ryan2019cln2inv`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11542)

## scope and result
CLN2INV learns explicit quantifier-free SMT loop invariants from execution traces. Its Continuous Logic Network (CLN) maps an SMT formula template to a differentiable computational graph, trains constants and predicate sharpness, then sends the discretized candidate to Z3. The abstract says: “we present Continuous Logic Network (CLN), a novel neural architecture for automatically learning loop invariants directly from program execution traces” (Abstract, p. 1). The target is invariant inference, not proof replacement: Z3 checks each learned candidate against the pre, post, and recursion conditions (Section 5, p. 7; Section 2.1, p. 3).

## technique 1: continuous semantic mapping for SMT

What it is. The paper defines a sound-and-complete semantic mapping `S` from quantifier-free SMT formulas to continuous truth values. It lists three desired properties: consistency with BL, differentiability almost everywhere, and increasing/decreasing everywhere as the terms approach constraint satisfaction/violation, which “ensures there is always a nonzero gradient for training” (Section 3, p. 4). For `>` and `≥`, shifted and scaled sigmoid functions are used (Section 3, p. 4); negation, conjunction, and disjunction come from BL t-norm/t-conorm choices, while `≤` and equality are derived (Section 3, p. 5).

When and steps.
1. Choose positive scale `B` and offset `ε`; map atomic inequalities with the paper's shifted sigmoid (Section 3, p. 4).
2. Derive `¬`, `∧`, and `∨` using one of the Łukasiewicz, Gödel, or product t-norms and their t-conorms (Section 3, p. 5; compared in Section 6.3 and Appendix G).
3. Derive `≤` from `≥` and negation, then equality as the conjunction of both inequalities (Section 3, p. 5).
4. Compose clause values recursively into the formula value `S(F)` and preserve the formula template's operators.

Verus form. Inference: do not replace Verus's Boolean specifications with fuzzy values. Keep a ghost SMT/real-valued model outside the verified Rust module, use it to propose constants, then emit a Boolean `spec fn` and check it with Verus `assert`, loop `invariant`, and `ensures`. The paper's differentiability is a training device, not a Verus proof rule.

Evidence and limits. The paper states soundness and completeness (Theorem 1, proved in Appendix B for quantifier-free linear formulas, as long as the t-norm satisfies Property 1; the product and Gödel t-norms have it, Łukasiewicz does not) and an optimality result for conjunctions of multiple linear equalities (Theorem 2, Appendix C, Section 4, p. 6). Theorem 3 (Appendix D) concerns the static-analysis strengthening of the precondition, not the mapping. The paper compares all three t-norm families (Section 6.3, p. 9, Table 2; Appendix G). The mapping covers quantifier-free formulas only (Section 3, p. 4); quantifiers and discontinuous predicates are outside the stated construction.

## technique 2: CLN construction and training

What it is. A CLN is built from an SMT formula template in which each term is marked as input, fixed constant, or learnable parameter. The graph is created recursively for terms and clauses. The paper states: “CLNs use our continuous semantic mapping to represent SMT formulas as an end-to-end differentiable model that can learn selected constants in the formula” (Section 4, p. 5).

When and steps.
1. Generate a template and mark learnable constants and predicate scales (Section 4, p. 5).
2. Construct the graph by recursively adding arithmetic operations, atomic predicates, and logical operations.
3. Run gradient optimization over valid assignments `X`, minimizing a loss that penalizes model outputs below one; add a hinge loss `max(0, β − B)` on the scaling factors and a squared-norm regularizer on the offset `ε` (Section 4, pp. 5–6).
4. Once the CLN is trained to a loss approaching 0, recover the SMT formula “by normalizing the learned parameters” and check it with Z3 (Section 5, p. 7).

Verus form. Inference: generate a candidate `spec fn` from the learned template, instantiate its constants, and have a Verus proof harness discharge the actual obligations. `assert` and `proof fn` lemmas replace Z3 calls only at the final Boolean-checking stage; the neural graph and optimizer remain untrusted tooling.

Evidence and limits. Training needs valid assignments, a chosen template, hyperparameters `λ` and `γ`, and a minimum scale `β` (Section 4, pp. 5–6). Inference: a low continuous loss is not itself a proof, and the recovered formula must still pass the solver check.

## technique 3: trace-driven invariant learning

What it is. CLN2INV runs the target program on random inputs satisfying preconditions, records variables before each loop execution and after termination, and learns an invariant template from those traces (Section 5, p. 6). Static analysis collects constants in the program code and the termination condition, and strengthens the precondition and weakens the postcondition “to constrain the problem as tightly as possible” (Section 5, pp. 6–7).

When and steps.
1. Sample random initial states: unconstrained variables use a uniform distribution, constrained variables use a uniform distribution inside the constraint (Section 5, p. 6).
2. Record loop-entry and final states.
3. Generate bounds on single variables (e.g., `t ≥ b`) and multivariable polynomial constraints (e.g., `w1 t1 + w2 t2 = b`), optionally with constants from static analysis and execution data; compose a template by selecting a subset of constraints and joining them with conjunctions or disjunctions (Section 5, p. 7).
4. Build and train the CLN on the traces, representing equality constraints as Gaussian-like functions (Section 5, p. 7; Appendix E), then recover the formula and ask Z3 to check it against the pre, post, and recursion conditions (Section 5, p. 7).
5. Training randomly restarts if the model does not reach termination within 2,000 epochs (Section 6, p. 7).

Verus form. Inference: use trace sampling only to propose a `spec fn` invariant. Encode that invariant in a Rust loop's `invariant` clauses and prove initialization, preservation, and exit postconditions over all inputs accepted by `requires`; sampled traces cannot establish universal correctness.

Evidence. On the Code2Inv benchmark, “CLN2INV is able to solve all 124 problems in the benchmark” and reports 1.1 seconds average in the conclusion (Sections 6.1 and 7, pp. 8–9; Table 1 gives 1.07 s). The comparison denominator is 124 theoretically solvable problems under a one-hour-per-problem limit; Code2Inv solved 90 and LoopInvGen 104 (Section 6.1, p. 8, Table 1). The nine removed programs are invalid because some inputs satisfy the precondition but violate the postcondition (Section 6, p. 7). Twelve additional difficult problems were all solved in under 20 seconds, while both baselines timed out after one hour (Section 6.2, p. 8). With training disabled, the static model solved 91 of 124 (Section 6.3, p. 8). The paper notes Code2Inv authors had reported 92, attributing the discrepancy to environment or randomized initialization (footnote 2, p. 8).

Limits. Inference: the benchmark and random traces are finite, so traces cannot cover all executions. The static-only ablation solving 91 of 124 shows that static heuristics already solve many problems, and that learning matters for the harder ones (Section 6.3, pp. 8–9). Inference: the 124/124 headline is a solver-checked benchmark result, not a proof that neural outputs are correct without Z3.

## techniques for the catalog
- continuous SMT semantics — map inequalities and Boolean connectives to differentiable truth values with scale and offset regularization
- formula-template CLN — compile a marked SMT template into a differentiable graph and learn selected constants
- trace-driven invariant inference — sample loop executions, statically generate terms, train a formula, discretize it, and submit it to a solver
- solver-in-the-loop filtering — retain only candidates that satisfy initiation, preservation, and postcondition checks
