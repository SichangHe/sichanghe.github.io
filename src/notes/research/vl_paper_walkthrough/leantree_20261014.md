# LeanTree: Accelerating White-Box Proof Search with Factorized States in Lean 4
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `kripner2025leantree`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14722v1)

## what the paper contributes
LeanTree is a Lean 4 interaction module and a dataset extractor for white-box theorem proving. Its central representation splits a proof state into goals that can be solved independently, while retaining goals coupled through a shared metavariable. The authors say the tool “factorizes complex proof states into simpler, independent branches” (abstract, p. 1). A tactic transforms open goals; a proof finishes only when no open goals remain (Section 3, pp. 3–4). Search over factorized states forms an AND-OR tree: an OR node chooses a tactic and an AND node requires every resulting goal (Section 3.2, p. 4).

## technique: factorized state search
Inference: use this when a verifier exposes multiple open goals and search can proceed on independent goals. The paper's procedure is: (1) start from a theorem and enter tactic mode, creating a metavariable and open goal (Section 3, p. 3); (2) apply a tactic to a proof state and receive resulting substates (Section 3.1, p. 3); (3) these substates factor the goals into independent groups, retaining coupled goals together (Sections 3.2–3.3, pp. 3–4); (4) search independent groups in any order, with factorization enabling state reuse and parallel search (Section 3.2, pp. 3–4); (5) require all goals to close. Their example applies `cases n <;> cases m` to `n * m = 0 ↔ n = 0 ∨ m = 0`, producing four goals that can be explored separately (Section 3.2, pp. 3–4). The paper states: “In contrast, factorized states allow the search algorithm to focus on the goals individually, in any order the algorithm deems fit.” (Section 3.2, p. 4).

Inference: for Verus, try representing independent obligations as separate lemmas with explicit contracts, and combine their postconditions in the caller. This proposed transfer would require evaluation; the paper studies Lean, not Verus.

## technique: detect metavariable coupling
Independence is conditional. Applying `Nat.le_trans` to `2 ≤ 5` creates goals `2 ≤ ?m` and `?m ≤ 5` sharing `?m`, plus a third goal of type `ℕ` for technical reasons; choosing a value for `?m` changes both inequalities (Section 3.3, p. 4). LeanTree detects all such dependencies and by default factorizes only as far as coupling permits. During extraction, sibling nodes sharing a metavariable are merged (Section 4.1.3, p. 6). In the Figure 2 example, assigning `?m` using `exact` makes the remaining two goals independent, so they can be split before being solved (Section 4.1, p. 4).

Inference: for Verus, test whether obligations share an unresolved choice that prevents independent proofs. Sharing a variable or predicate alone does not establish the Lean-style coupling described here.

## technique: normalize proofs into small transitions
LeanTree extracts existing proofs in three stages: singleton-tree construction, tactic simplification, and merging metavariable-dependent sibling goals (Sections 4.1.1–4.1.3, pp. 5–6). Singleton-tree construction matches tactic applications to affected goals (Section 4.1.1, p. 5). Simplification replaces nested tactic blocks with placeholders and child goals, separates `cases` and `induction` branches, and partitions merged `rw` commands; `rwa` additionally needs an `assumption` step to preserve semantics (Section 4.1.2, p. 5). A synthetic `case succ n’` step gives a name to an otherwise inaccessible constructor variable (Section 4.1.2, pp. 5–6). Finally, sibling nodes sharing a metavariable are merged, and the whole tree is executed in Lean REPL so every branch ends proved (Section 4.1.3, p. 6).

Inference: for Verus, normalize an agent-generated proof into small `proof fn` lemmas and `assert`/`calc` steps, preserving dependencies in a manifest. Do not claim that this extraction is automatic in Verus; the paper implements it for Lean syntax and PaperProof.

## technique: incremental verification and false-positive prevention
The authors report finding 12 incorrect proofs in the MiniF2F validation set that the old Lean REPL accepted without kernel-checking tactic assignments (Appendix B, p. 11). The paper gives a concrete `apply?` hazard: “However, the apply? tactic must not be left in a final proof, as it has the same semantics as the sorry keyword, leaving such a proof incomplete.” (Appendix A, p. 11). LeanTree bans `apply?` by default, with explicit opt-in (Appendix A, p. 11). Its revised verifier checks only new assignments introduced by a tactic, rather than the whole proof term. The authors argue this avoids false negatives from syntactic branching and reduces verification complexity from quadratic to linear because each sub-assignment is checked once (Appendix B, pp. 11–12). The authors state that all proofs found by their prover are independently checked directly by Lean (Section 3.4, p. 4; Appendix B, p. 12).

Inference: a Verus product should reject proof escape hatches and run Verus plus independent tests or a second checker where feasible. The exact incremental assignment algorithm is Lean-specific; Verus's ordinary function contracts and solver checks do not provide this paper's REPL behavior.

## evidence, limits, and implications for VL
Each dataset sample represents a Lean file, containing theorems and their tactic proofs. It records imports, open namespaces, source character offsets, and proof-tree size and depth for potential critic-model objectives (Section 4.2, pp. 6–7; Appendix D, p. 13). LeanTree reports 74,706 factorized Mathlib tactic proofs and 26,201 DeepSeek-Prover-V1 proofs. Conversion failed for 23.0% of Mathlib tactic proofs and 4.7% of DeepSeek-Prover-V1 tactic proofs; 28.6% of Mathlib failures came from `calc` and `conv` (Section 4.2, p. 7). In five-run MiniF2F-test experiments with Llemma-7B, whole-proof generation reached 9.59% ± 0.71, black-box rollout 5.32% ± 0.37, and white-box rollout 18.36% ± 0.60; best-first search reported by Azerbayev et al. (2023b) reached 26.23% (Figure 3, p. 8). For linear rollouts, each input theorem received ten rollouts of at most 25 tactics; success required a valid proof from any rollout. The ± values are standard deviations across five runs. All experiments together used one node with eight AMD MI210 accelerators and what the paper calls “192 CPU nodes,” totaling 59 GPU-hours and 450 CPU-hours (Section 6, p. 8). Appendix C (p. 12) says they selected only the first tactic from each model output and disabled sampling of `sorry`, `admit`, and comment syntax. The paper does not state the MiniF2F-test subset size in this experiment; its benchmark overview gives 488 problems for MiniF2F overall (Section 2, p. 3).

The paper checks extracted trees by replaying them in Lean REPL and discards trees that fail (Section 4.1.3, p. 6); it independently verifies proofs found by its prover directly in Lean (Appendix B, p. 12). Its experimental conclusion is limited: “Our results show that in our specific setting, supplying the prover with internal proof states improves proof search performance.” (Section 6, p. 8). Inference: these results motivate testing coupling-aware decomposition on Verus contracts and measuring proof repair cost under Rust changes. They do not establish a Verus benefit or measure the contribution of factorization separately from intermediate-state feedback.

## techniques for the catalog
- factorized AND-OR search — split independent verifier goals and solve all branches with reuse and parallel search
- coupling-aware splitting — retain goals sharing unresolved metavariables together, then split when assignment removes the dependency
- proof-tree normalization — convert nested, branched, and composite tactics into small executable proof transitions
- incremental kernel checking — check newly assigned proof material and independently recheck completed proofs
- unsafe-tactic filtering — ban library-search placeholders such as `apply?` that behave like unfinished proofs
