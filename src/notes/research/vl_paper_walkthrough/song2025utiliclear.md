# Assuring Certified Database Utility in Privacy-Preserving Database Fingerprinting
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `song2025utiliclear`, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/song-mingyang)

## problem and result
UtiliClear lets a database recipient verify a bound on fingerprint-induced bit modifications while the original database and modification positions remain hidden. The paper states, “This paper, for the first time, addresses the research gap in providing recipients the ability to verify the maximum number of modified bits in database fingerprinting” (abstract, PDF p. 2). The actors are a database owner (DO), who embeds recipient-specific fingerprints, and recipients, who need utility assurance; the threat model treats both as untrusted (section 3.2, PDF p. 5). A zero-knowledge proof over the whole embedding circuit is called impractical: extrapolating from the cited ZKP's measurements on an Amazon EC2 m5.4xlarge instance, the paper estimates about 1000 hours of computation, 32 TB of bandwidth, and 400 MB of memory to process a 34 GB database of about 230 million records (introduction, PDF p. 3). UtiliClear instead combines a fuzzy perturbation verification protocol (FPV), Pedersen commitments, Goldwasser–Micali (GM) encryption, and the existing fingerprint embedding method of Ji et al. (NDSS 2023, the paper's reference [18]).

## technique: FPV bound on hidden bit changes
what it is
FPV verifies whether the Hamming distance between an original bit-string x and a modified x′ is at most a public threshold t, without revealing x or the differing positions to the verifier. Its core maps the differences onto an error-correcting-code (ECC) codeword via the embedded error codeword “cw′ = x ⊕ x′ ⊕ cw”; if decoding cw′ returns the verifier's random message, at most t bits differ (section 4.1, PDF p. 6). Security is defined by fuzzy-binding (a prover passes with distance >t only with negligible probability) and full-hiding (the verifier cannot identify differing positions) (Definition 2, section 3.4, PDF p. 6).

when to use it
Use FPV when a prover supplies a modified bit-string but the verifier must check a modification budget while the source and change locations stay confidential. The paper applies it to fingerprinted data whose utility bound is expressed as a maximum number of changed insignificant bits.

how to do it
1. Setup: the prover runs GM key generation and publishes pk while retaining sk; n and t are public (section 4.2, PDF p. 7).
2. Lock: the prover encrypts x as Cx=GM.Enc(x,pk) and sends it. The verifier samples random u and permutation seed δ, computes Cu=GM.Enc(u,pk) and Cy=Perm(Cx◦Cu,δ) (◦ is the Hadamard product, which XORs plaintexts under GM), keeps vp=⟨u,δ⟩, and sends Cy; u and δ are not reused across executions. The prover decrypts y=GM.Dec(Cy,sk)=Perm(x⊕u,δ) (Figure 2 and section 4.2, PDF p. 7).
3. Challenge: after receiving x′, the verifier samples m ∈ {0,1}^k, encodes cw=EC_n,t(m), computes z=Perm(x′⊕u,δ)⊕cw, and sends z (Figure 3 and section 4.2, PDF p. 7).
4. Proof: the prover computes cw′=y⊕z, decodes m′=DC_n,t(cw′), and sends m′ to the verifier (Figure 3, PDF p. 7).
5. Check: the verifier returns 1 exactly when m′=m (Figure 3, PDF p. 7). Correctness follows from Dis(cw,cw′)=Dis(Perm(x,δ),Perm(x′,δ))=Dis(x,x′) (section 4.4, PDF p. 8).

The ECC must tolerate t errors, and the paper requires a decoder that fails silently: “The FPV employs an ECC scheme that cannot verify whether decoding succeeds or not, and outputs a random result when the number of errors exceed the error tolerance” (section 4.2, PDF p. 7). The verifier's random perturbation keeps the prover from building cw′ from a string with at most t modifications while submitting an x′ with more (section 4.1, PDF p. 6). The paper also notes the verifier could manipulate the perturbation to learn x, and says the prover can counter this by comparing statistical properties such as counts of 1's and 0's (section 4.2, PDF p. 8).

verus form
Inference: implement deterministic ECC encode/decode, XOR, Hamming-distance lemmas, and permutation invariants as Verus `spec fn`, executable functions with `requires`/`ensures`, and proof functions. Model encryption and zero-knowledge hiding as abstract specifications or externally trusted cryptographic primitives; Verus can prove the protocol's algebraic relation only under their stated contracts. Keep secret keys and ciphertexts in ordinary Rust values; proof code must not be called at runtime. A Verus `lemma` is a proof function, and loop invariants belong on loops rather than as a call named `invariant`.

evidence
Figure 4 walks through a (5,1)-FPV on a five-bit string with one modified bit (section 4.3, PDF p. 8), ending in m′=m. Theorem 1 states FPV is secure if GM encryption is semantically secure (section 4.4, PDF p. 9). The fuzzy-binding argument rests on the perturbation's security against the prover, proved as an indistinguishability game for uniformly random u and δ (Theorem 4, Appendix B.1, PDF p. 17); full-hiding rests on GM semantic security (PDF p. 9). Inference: the proofs are pen-and-paper and also depend on the silent-failure ECC behavior quoted above; no machine-checked proof is reported. The paper reports no standalone FPV benchmark; FPV cost appears only inside the UtiliClear measurements.

## technique: UtiliClear database construction
what it is
UtiliClear divides each attribute column into ng groups, splits each attribute value into significant and insignificant bits, commits to the significant bits, and runs FPV on each group's insignificant bits. The paper says, “significant bits are crucial for maintaining data utility, fingerprints are typically embedded by modifying insignificant bits” (introduction, PDF p. 3). Embedding and extraction follow Ji et al. [18]; UtiliClear adds a per-group modification limit and the verification (section 5.1, PDF p. 10).

how to do it
1. Setup: the DO runs PC.Setup and FPV.Setup and sends pp and pk to the recipient; the recipient generates a signature key pair and sends pksig to the DO (section 5.1, PDF p. 10).
2. Preprocess: the recipient chooses ng, t, and insignificant-bit positions P. The DO assigns each attribute value to group S1(significant bits) mod ng + 1, commits each significant-bit group with PC.Com(H1(SC[i][j]),γ,pp) and sends Ψ and OP; DO and recipient then run FPV.Lock on each insignificant-bit group, giving Φ and VP (Figure 5 and section 5.1, PDF p. 10).
3. Embed: the recipient picks random ζ, signs ID‖ζ, and sends ζ, ID, and the signature; the DO verifies it, sets f=H2(ID‖ζ), and embeds fingerprint bits as in [18], with a counter matrix M (initialized to t) stopping changes once a group has used its t modifiable bits (section 5.1, PDF p. 10; Figure 10, Appendix A, PDF p. 17).
4. Verify: the recipient regroups the fingerprinted database, runs PC.Open on each significant-bit group and FPV.Verify with the DO on each insignificant-bit group, and accepts only if all groups pass (Figure 6 and section 5.1, PDF p. 11).
5. Extract: the DO uses sk and the original and fingerprinted databases to recompute fingerprint positions and majority-vote each fingerprint bit (section 5.1, PDF p. 11; Figure 11, PDF p. 17).

Inference: in Verus, group bounds become quantified sequence properties and per-group lemmas; commitments, signatures, and GM encryption remain trusted contracts unless separately verified.

evidence and limits
Theorem 2 states modification extent verification holds if FPV is secure and the Pedersen commitment is binding; Theorem 3 states fingerprint positions stay confidential under the quadratic residuosity assumption; Theorem 5 states ε-entry-level differential privacy under a parameter condition (section 5.2, PDF p. 11; Appendix B, PDF pp. 17–20). The implementation is C++ with OpenSSL and GMP on two desktops with Intel Core i9-13900 processors and 16 GB memory. Datasets are the Amazon Review dataset (DB3, 34 GB, 230,139,802 records) and subsets DB1 (8 GB, 54,846,036 records) and DB2 (16 GB, 109,693,107 records). FPV uses Reed–Solomon with n=2040, t=128, and k=1784; the last d=2, 3, or 4 bits are insignificant; κ=256 and fingerprint length 128 (section 6.1, PDF p. 12). The paper states, “This ECC does not support decoding result verification” (section 6.1, PDF p. 12). Computation and communication costs are plotted against group number and d in Figures 7–9 (PDF pp. 12–13). Memory is reported as an average of 580 MB for the DO and 620 MB for the recipient on DB3 (section 6.2, PDF p. 13; Table 2, PDF p. 14). Appendices C and D report fingerprint robustness and query/classification utility comparable to [18] at similar modification extents, with robustness above SJJ18 (PDF pp. 20–21). The paper states, “UtiliClear does not protect the privacy of data providers against the DO” (section 5.2, PDF p. 12). Inference: utility is certified only as a count of changed insignificant bits; the paper shows accuracy falling as modifications rise but does not prove accuracy for arbitrary analytics.

## what is proved, trusted, and relevant to VL
The formal claims are FPV fuzzy-binding and full-hiding (Theorem 1), UtiliClear modification extent verification (Theorem 2), fingerprint-position confidentiality (Theorem 3), and entry-level differential privacy (Theorem 5); the evidence is pen-and-paper proofs plus large-database measurements. Inference: trusted components include cryptographic hardness (GM semantic security, quadratic residuosity, Pedersen binding), random generators, ECC behavior beyond its correction radius, implementation correctness, and the negotiated significance partition. Inference: for agent-written verified Rust, the direct threat is an unverified cryptographic or serialization boundary that violates these contracts while Verus proves only local XOR/count arithmetic. Inference: the paper already supplies a practical hidden modification-budget protocol, so VL should reuse its decomposition rather than claim novelty for the same combination.

## techniques for the catalog
- fuzzy perturbation verification — hide a source bit-string with homomorphic random perturbation and use an ECC challenge to test a Hamming-distance budget
- groupwise utility certification — commit significant bits and FPV-check insignificant bits independently for scalable database processing
- privacy-preserving fingerprint embedding — embed recipient fingerprints in insignificant bits while retaining extraction and modification bounds
