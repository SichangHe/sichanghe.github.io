# Constraint-based Relational Verification
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `unno2021constraint`, [paper](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02628v1)

## problem and result

The paper extends constrained Horn clauses (CHCs) to relational properties that need more than ordinary safety: k-safety, co-termination, termination-sensitive non-interference (TS-NI), and generalized non-interference (GNI). Its language, pfwCSP, allows arbitrary clauses, ordinary predicates, well-founded predicates, and functional predicates. The abstract states: "Our approach enables us to express and automatically verify problem instances that require non-trivial (i.e., non-sequential and non-lock-step) self-composition by automatically inferring appropriate schedulers (or alignment) that dictate when and which program copies move." (Abstract, p. 1). PCSat encodes a relational problem as pfwCSP and solves it with stratified counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS).

## technique 1: semantic scheduler synthesis for k-safety

What it is. A k-safety property relates k executions: a pre-relation on initial states must imply a post-relation on final states. The paper's `doubleSquare` example needs a semantic schedule because lock-step execution cannot express a useful linear invariant: the copy with `h=false` must execute its loop twice for every iteration of the `h=true` copy (§2.1, pp. 3–4). pfwCSP introduces an ordinary predicate `inv` for a relational invariant and one ordinary predicate `schA` for each nonempty subset `A` of program copies. `schA` says that exactly copies in `A` step while the others remain still (§4.1, pp. 9–10).

How to apply it. (1) Define each program's one-step transition `Ti` and final-state predicate `Fi`. (2) Define `Pre` and `Post` over the tuple of program variables. (3) Add `Pre ⇒ inv`. (4) Add `inv ∧ all Fi ⇒ Post`. (5) For every nonempty `A`, require that `inv ∧ schA`, the selected transitions, and stuttering of unselected copies imply `inv` at the next tuple. (6) Add scheduler-fairness clauses: if some program is unfinished, a selected set includes an unfinished program; and every invariant state with an unfinished program satisfies some `schA` (§4.1, Definition 3, pp. 9–10). Solve for both `inv` and the scheduler predicates. The authors' example infers predicates that schedule the `h=true` copy twice per `h=false` loop iteration (Example 1, p. 10).

Why fairness matters. Without fairness, a scheduler could leave one nonterminated copy forever while advancing another, making the invariant vacuously useful. The paper states: "The soundness direction crucially relies on scheduler fairness." (Theorem 1, §4.1, p. 10). The authors prove that the constraint set is satisfiable exactly when the k-safety property holds, relative to semantic predicate solutions; syntactic completeness depends on the background theory expressing the needed predicates (§4.1, p. 10).

Verus form. Inference: encode each Rust execution as a ghost state machine or duplicated specification state, and represent `inv` as a `spec fn` relation over the states. A scheduler can be a ghost `spec fn` deciding which copy advances; proof code must establish an invariant for every permitted transition and a fairness or progress lemma. Verus state-machine and `invariant` constructs can express the transition relation, but Verus does not automatically synthesize a semantic scheduler. The scheduler search and self-composition would be an external generator; the emitted Rust proof still needs `requires`, `ensures`, and invariant proofs.

## technique 2: well-founded constraints for co-termination

Co-termination asks whether, for every pre-related pair, termination of one program implies termination of the other. Ordinary scheduler fairness is insufficient because one nonterminating copy can be scheduled indefinitely. pfwCSP therefore marks a predicate variable `wfr` as well-founded and uses a functional predicate `fnb` to choose a step-difference bound `b` (§4.2, pp. 10–11). The relation `wfr` witnesses eventual progress of the lagging program.

How to apply it. (1) Introduce an invariant `inv(d,b,V1,V2)` where `d` is the difference in steps between copies and `b` is a bound. (2) Use a functional `fnb(V1,V2,b)` to initialize `d=0` and choose `b`. (3) Constrain `-b ≤ d ≤ b` while both copies are unfinished. (4) Provide scheduler cases `schTT`, `schFT`, and `schTF`; update `d` when only one copy steps and preserve `inv`. (5) Add fairness clauses. (6) When copy 1 is final and copy 2 is not, require every copy-2 transition to satisfy `wfr` on its old and new states (§4.2, Definition 5, pp. 11–12). A symmetric property is obtained by exchanging the programs. The paper's PCSat solution discovers a schedule, relational invariant, and well-founded relations for the example, including the condition that if one loop has terminated, either the other has terminated or its parameter is bounded away from the problematic value (§2.1, pp. 4–5).

Verus form. Inference: express `wfr` with a Verus `spec fn` relation and prove well-founded descent through a `decreases` clause or ranking lemma on each transition. A ghost integer can track step difference, and a ghost function can choose its bound. Verus's termination checker proves a particular executable recursion or loop decreases; it does not natively quantify over two arbitrary program executions, so a self-composed ghost model and explicit relational lemmas are required.

## technique 3: prophecy and functional predicates for GNI

GNI is a ∀∃ hyperproperty: for every move of one program there must exist a matching move of the other, or (for TI-GNI) a nonterminating execution. The encoding handles demonic choices universally and angelic choices with functional predicates. For the demonic side, prophecy variables guess final outputs; for the angelic side, a functional predicate chooses a total assignment to the nondeterministic choice (§2.1, pp. 5–6; §4.3, pp. 12–14).

How to apply it. (1) Replace the first program's variables by prophecy variables plus its state, leave prophecy values unconstrained in `Pre`, and carry them unchanged through transitions. (2) Require the prophecy to equal the first program's final output before enforcing `Post` (TI-GNI) or before enforcing both termination and `Post` (TS-GNI). (3) Factor the second program's nondeterministic transition as `U2(r,V2,V2')`, with explicit choice `r`. (4) Add a functional predicate `fnr(prophecy,V2,r)` so the solver selects a total angelic choice depending on the demonic final output. (5) For TS-GNI, add co-termination's difference-bound and well-founded constraints (§4.3, Definitions 7–8, pp. 12–14). Finite nondeterminism can instead use a disjunction of invariant successors; infinite choice needs a functional predicate (Remark 3, p. 14).

The authors' `gniEx` contains an integer nondeterministic choice and a Boolean loop choice. PCSat discovers the function `r = ret1`, where `ret1` is a prophecy variable, and verifies both TI-GNI and TS-GNI (§2.1, p. 6). The paper proves soundness by determinizing angelic choices with the synthesized functions and reducing to k-safety/co-termination, and completeness by synthesizing choice functions from executions (§4.3, p. 14).

Verus form. Inference: represent prophecy values and angelic choices as ghost variables in a self-composed state machine. A `spec fn` can state the chosen relation, while `proof fn` lemmas show that every demonic execution has a matching ghost choice and that final outputs satisfy the relation. Executable Rust cannot rely on ghost nondeterminism; the construction verifies a model or wrapper contract. The total-function and well-founded obligations must be proved explicitly.

## technique 4: stratified CEGIS for mutually dependent witnesses

The solver searches simultaneously for ordinary invariants, well-founded relations, and functional predicates. Each CEGIS iteration has a synthesis phase and a validation phase (§5, pp. 14–15). Synthesis finds a candidate satisfying the current ground examples; validation uses an SMT solver to find violating substitutions. Violating clauses are instantiated and added as new examples. If the current template has no solution, an unsat core identifies parameters to enlarge.

The key anti-overfitting device is a stratified family of templates. Ordinary predicates start with small disjunctive affine inequalities; well-founded predicates use piecewise lexicographic affine ranking functions; functional predicates use piecewise affine functions. Parameters such as numbers of disjuncts, conjuncts, regions, and coefficient bounds are increased fairly and on demand (§5.1, pp. 15–17). The paper says: "Initially, we assign each predicate variable a less expressive template and gradually refine it in a counterexample-guided manner." (§5.1, p. 16). Because each stratum is finite and parameters grow fairly, a syntactic solution expressible in the family is eventually found (Theorem 5, §5.1, p. 17).

How to apply it. (1) Instantiate small templates for every predicate kind. (2) Solve the current ground examples with QFLIA SMT. (3) Validate the candidate against all original clauses. (4) Add one grounded counterexample per violated clause. (5) If unsatisfiable, inspect the unsat core and increase the implicated template parameters, fairly balancing dependent predicates. (6) Repeat until a solution or genuine unsatisfiable example set is returned (§5, pp. 14–17). The parameter-update strategy itself is partly manual; the paper leaves automatic tuning as future work (§5.1, p. 17).

Verus form. Inference: a VL synthesizer could generate candidate `spec fn` invariants, ranking functions, and ghost choice functions, then ask Verus/Z3 to validate them and use failing proof obligations as counterexamples. The stratified templates must emit valid Verus syntax and respect ownership and integer semantics. Verus can check each candidate, but it does not provide this CEGIS loop or unsat-core-guided template growth as a built-in feature.

## evidence, trust, and limits

PCSat is an OCaml implementation using Z3, Boolean formulas, and quantifier-free linear integer/rational inequalities. The 20-problem benchmark ran on a 3.1 GHz Intel Xeon Platinum 8000 with 32 GiB RAM and a 600 s limit (§6, p. 17). It solved 15 problems fully automatically and 5 semi-automatically; four needed manually supplied invariant hints and two needed manually chosen initial template parameters (Table 1 and §6, pp. 17–19). The table has 20 named problems; each time and iteration count is per problem, not a percentage. The paper reports that suitable functional predicates supplied manually make the GNI problems solvable in under 10 s, identifying functional-predicate synthesis as the current bottleneck (§6, p. 19).

The framework trusts the transition predicates, background theory, Z3, template encoding, and the manual reduction of programs to pfwCSP. It proves satisfiability sound and complete relative to semantic predicate interpretations, but syntactic completeness depends on template expressiveness. Arrays are outside the current full support: the authors omit `ArrayIntMod` because it requires a theory of arrays (§6, footnote 7, p. 18). Existing work already covers self-composition, product programs, fixed schedules, and CHC solving; the new contribution is their combination with inferred semantic scheduling, well-foundedness, functionality, and stratified CEGIS for infinite-state non-k-safety properties.

Inference: threat to agent-written verified Rust is high for relational specifications but low for ordinary single-run safety. An agent could use a synthesized relational invariant or scheduler to compare two versions, executions, or security modes, but mistakes in the model of nondeterminism or fairness can invalidate the intended property while the generated constraints remain satisfiable. VL should preserve the generated transition model, prophecy semantics, and solver certificate, and should require a human-defined `Pre`/`Post` contract for security claims.

## techniques for the catalog

- semantic scheduler synthesis — infer state-dependent schedules and relational invariants for k-safety with fair non-lock-step execution
- well-founded co-termination encoding — combine step-difference bounds, fairness, and a well-founded relation to prove paired termination
- prophecy and functional predicates — encode ∀∃ non-interference by guessing final outputs and synthesizing total angelic choices
- stratified CEGIS — grow ordinary, ranking, and functional templates from counterexamples and unsat cores
