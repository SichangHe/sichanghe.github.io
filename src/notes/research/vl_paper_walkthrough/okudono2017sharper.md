# Sharper and Simpler Nonlinear Interpolants for Program Verification
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `okudono2017sharper`, [paper](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00314v1)

The paper synthesizes polynomial interpolants for jointly infeasible nonlinear predicates, improving sharpness and coefficient simplicity. Its abstract says the method combines “real algebraic geometry and SDP optimization” and adds “our continued fraction-based algorithm that rounds off (potentially erroneous) numerical solutions of SDP solvers.” The abstract says interpolation “plays important roles in various program verification techniques such as invariant synthesis and CEGAR.”

## technique: strict-cone Positivstellensatz interpolation
Use this when two semialgebraic regions are disjoint but barely so: replacing a strict inequality by a non-strict one makes their closures intersect. Represent each region as an SAS with non-strict inequalities, strict `>`, and equalities. Replace the earlier disequality-based certificate with a strict cone, closed under addition, multiplication, and positive constants. The paper’s theorem says a system is infeasible if and only if there exist `f` in the cone generated by the non-strict and strict polynomials (`C(f1,…,fs,g1,…,gt)`), `g` in the strict cone `SC(g1,…,gt)`, and `h` in the ideal of the equalities such that `f + g + h = 0` (Theorem 3.7, p.11). For the original Theorem 2.4 the paper says the “only if” direction “is however not used in the algorithm of [8] nor in this paper” (p.6).

To synthesize an interpolant, search for certificates for both regions with the shared-variable coefficient constraints, then return `S := (f + g + h > 0)` (Theorem 3.8, p.11). The certificate condition is `f + f' + g + h + h' = 0`, where the primed polynomials come from the second region `T'`. The paper states the method is a strict generalization in the sense that if Dai et al.’s Algorithm 1 finds an interpolant for disjoint systems, Algorithm 2 also does after translation (Section 3.2 end, p.13; Appendix C). Algorithm 2 performs this search: choose a maximum degree `b`, solve the extended `PDiophSOS` constraints for sum-of-squares coefficients, polynomial multipliers, and nonnegative strict-cone coefficients, enforce `sum γk ≥ 1`, cancel non-shared monomials, and construct `S` (Algorithm 2, p.12). The strict cone allows a polynomial `g` where the earlier method used `g^2`, which “yields an interpolant of a potentially smaller degree” (Remark 3.11, p.12). Example 3.10 (p.12) takes `T = (−y > 0)` and `T' = (y − x ≥ 0, y + x ≥ 0)`, which are only barely disjoint, and derives `−2y > 0`. Proposition 3.3 (p.10) shows the earlier certificate form can never exist for barely disjoint systems.

Inference: In Verus, an interpolant can become a candidate `invariant` or lemma over integer or real mathematical values. Verus does not provide Positivstellensatz or SDP synthesis, so this is an external predicate generator whose result must be encoded as a proved `ensures`/`invariant`. Machine integer overflow and nonlinear arithmetic support require separate modeling.

## technique: SDP relaxation and coefficient constraints
Reduce the certificate search to semidefinite programming. The paper says the problem in Algorithm 2, Line 3 “can also be reduced to an SDP problem,” following [8, 24, 25] (p.12). Enforce the common-variable condition by requiring the coefficient of each monomial that involves variables other than the shared ones `X` to be 0; “The constraint is linear in the SDP variables” (Section 3.2, pp.12–13). The implementation uses SDPT3 through YALMIP (Section 4, p.13); the tool is written in OCaml and generates MATLAB code (Section 4, p.14).

Inference: Sum-of-squares polynomials are standardly encoded as positive-semidefinite Gram-matrix constraints; the paper defers this to the cited works and does not spell it out.

## technique: rounding-validation loop
Numerical SDP output can violate exact constraints and has unreadable floating coefficients. Apply extended continued-fraction rounding to coefficient ratios, starting at depth `d=1` for the simplest candidate; before rounding, the original candidate is rounded to `c` decimal places (the “precision”, an “empirical implementation trick,” Section 4, p.14). Validate each rounded candidate symbolically against the original certificate constraints. If validation fails, increase `d`; larger depth approaches the SDP solution. Rounding converges to the original ratio for large enough `d` (Lemma 4.1); if no candidate validates by then, the tool returns `FAIL` (Fig. 1, p.14). The workflow is adopted from Harrison [12]. The paper states: “In each iteration we check if the candidate v d yields a valid interpolant. This validation phase is conducted purely symbolically, ensuring soundness of our tool.” (Section 4, p.14). Validation checks equalities exactly and positive semidefiniteness via “all the principal minors of M are nonnegative” (Section 4.2, p.15). The relaxation `sum γk ≥ 1` is wound back to the original `> 0` during validation (footnote 6, p.15).

## evidence, trust, and limits
On a MacBook Pro 2.7 GHz with 16 GB, reported times are averages of 10 runs (Section 5, p.16). Table 1 has 9 geometric cases: 8 produce interpolants and one returns `FAIL`; times range from 2.19 to 43.7 seconds. A CEGAR example (Listing 1.1) synthesizes `189346(xa)+378692(ya) ≥ 0`, equivalent to `(xa)+2(ya) ≥ 0`, in 4.32 seconds with `b=0,c=5`, then proves safety after adding it as an abstraction predicate (Section 5, pp.16–18). A second, constant-acceleration example (Listing 1.2) needs predicates `2x ≥ 0` and `8v ≥ 0`, with 1.92 s (`b=0,c=5,d=1`) and 2.87 s synthesis times (Section 5, p.18). In the first CEGAR example aiSat cannot interpolate the barely disjoint pair; after the authors strengthened `T'` to `(xa)+2(ya) ≤ −10^−7`, aiSat succeeded but its predicate “cannot exclude the spurious path p1”; the second example is similar (Section 5, p.18). For the infeasibility-checking example the paper reports its interpolant `8 − 14(ya1) − 7(xa1) ≥ 0` (14.1 s, `b=0,c=3,d=11`) is “far simpler” than the one in [8] (Section 5, p.16). For input 6 of Table 1 the tool returned `FAIL` for all parameters tried (p.16).

Inference: Trusted components are the symbolic validation, the SDP encoding, the real-arithmetic semantics, and the implementation. The paper says soundness is ensured by the symbolic validation phase. The method can return `FAIL` (Algorithm 2, Fig. 1; Table 1 input 6). Disjunctions are not allowed in the input form and are handled by splitting into pairs of disjuncts, as in [8, §3.1] (Section 5, p.16). The paper’s program examples are small programs over `real` variables (Listings 1.1, 1.2); no Rust or machine-integer evidence is supplied. The authors write: “Our current examples are rather simple and small... practical applicability of the method in the context of program verification is yet to be confirmed” (Introduction, Future Work, p.5).

## techniques for the catalog
- strict-cone interpolation: derive sharp polynomial interpolants from Positivstellensatz certificates with strict inequalities
- SDP certificate search: encode sum-of-squares and shared-variable conditions as semidefinite constraints
- rounding-validation loop: simplify numerical candidates with continued fractions and accept only symbolic validation
