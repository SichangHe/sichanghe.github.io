# Effective Attacks and Provable Defenses for Website Fingerprinting
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `wang2014effective`, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity14/technical-sessions/presentation/wang_tao)

## scope and result

This paper gives two connected techniques. First, it builds a strong passive website-fingerprinting attack with k-nearest neighbours (k-NN), a large feature vector, and learned feature weights. Second, it defines a defense whose privacy can be bounded against every classifier, then minimizes bandwidth for that defense with shortest common supersequences. The formal-verification lesson is the second technique: prove a property about all attacks by reasoning from the complete defended observation set, rather than testing a few attacks. The paper states, “To defend against such attacks, we need provably effective defenses.” (Abstract, proceedings p. 143).

The result is model-relative. The defense assumes simulatable, deterministic packet transformation, a specified testing multiset, and a chosen anonymity-set information level. The paper's practical evaluator still uses an oracle for page-load boundaries and excludes other user activity (Section 8.1, p. 154). Inference: a Verus proof of the bound would certify the stated finite model and transformation, not real-world traffic outside those assumptions.

## technique: feature-weighted k-NN attack as an adversarial test

What it is. A packet trace records packet lengths, directions, order and timing. The classifier computes a weighted L1 distance over roughly 4,000 features. The paper says, “The total number of features is close to 4,000 (3,000 of which are just for the unique packet lengths).” (Section 4.2, p. 147) and then labels a test trace by the class shared by its k nearest training points. Features include total size and time, packet counts, unique lengths, ordering, concentrations, bursts, and the first 20 packet lengths (Section 4.2, pp. 146–147). Missing variable-length features are padded with `X`; any comparison involving `X` contributes zero difference (Section 4.2, p. 147).

When to use it. Use this as a stress test when a defense claims to hide packet sequence information. The attack intentionally learns which features remain distinguishable after a defense. It handles multi-modal page classes because nearest neighbours need only match one mode, not a single class centroid (Sections 2.2 and 4.1, pp. 144–146).

How to apply it. (1) Gather labelled training traces and held-out test traces; keep monitored and non-monitored pages separate (Sections 2.2 and 5.1, pp. 144, 148–149). (2) Compute the feature vector and start weights uniformly at random in [0.5, 1.5] (Section 4.3, p. 148). (3) For each training point, find the `kreco` nearest same-class points and nearest other-class points. Count, per feature, how many bad distances are no larger than the worst good distance (Section 4.3, p. 148). (4) Reduce weights whose `nbad` differs from the minimum, initially by 1% of the weight and then scaling the reduction by `nbad/kreco`; increase the minimum-`nbad` weights equally so total bad-class distance stays fixed (Section 4.3, p. 148). Also scale reductions by `0.2 + Nbad/kreco`, where `Nbad` measures how poorly the point is classified (p. 148). (5) Repeat for R rounds, then classify a test trace only when all k neighbours agree on one monitored page; otherwise classify it as non-monitored (Section 5.1, p. 149). (6) Report true-positive and false-positive rates with the monitored/non-monitored denominator and cross-validation split.

Evidence. On 100 monitored pages with 90 instances each and 5,000 non-monitored pages with one instance each, training uses 60 monitored instances and tests the remaining 30 with all-but-one cross-validation (Section 5.1, p. 149). The attack reaches TPR 0.85 ± 0.04 at FPR 0.006 ± 0.004; OSAD reaches TPR 0.83 ± 0.03 at FPR 0.06 ± 0.02 on the same open-world setting (p. 149). Weight training takes about 120 CPU seconds for 1,000 rounds and 5,000 non-monitored pages, versus about 1,600 CPU hours for OSAD and 500 CPU hours for Cai et al.; testing one instance takes about 0.1 CPU seconds versus 800 and 450 (Section 5.1, p. 150). These are the reported hardware and dataset settings, not general complexity guarantees.

Verus form. Inference: represent a trace as a verified sequence and define a `spec fn` for each feature and the weighted distance. A `proof fn` can establish that the chosen neighbour class satisfies a stated tie-breaking rule and that weight updates preserve nonnegativity or a fixed total distance. Use `requires` for trace-format and training-set assumptions and `ensures` for the classifier result. The paper's floating-point training and empirical rates do not carry over as exact Verus facts without a fixed integer or rational model. The attack itself is an adversarial test harness, not a proof that a product is private.

## technique: an attacker upper bound from possibility sets

What it is. For a deterministic defense D and testing multiset S, the observation p maps to a possibility multiset Q(p) containing the classes of all traces that produce p. Even an attacker trained on the entire testing set can be wrong only when the same observation corresponds to multiple classes. The optimal attacker chooses the most frequent class in Q(p), giving `Acc(p) = max-class-count / |Q(p)|` (Section 6.1, pp. 151–152). Overall non-uniform accuracy is the mean of Acc(p), while uniform accuracy is the maximum over observations (p. 152).

How to apply it. (1) Fix the defended testing multiset, including repeated observation/class pairs (Section 6.1, p. 151). (2) Apply D to every trace and group equal observations. (3) For each group, count class multiplicities and compute the most frequent-class fraction. (4) Take the maximum for a uniform bound or mean for non-uniform accuracy (p. 152). (5) State the prior and whether the bound is over the training/testing multiset; do not claim a population guarantee from a finite set.

Verus form. Inference: model traces, classes, and the defense as finite sequences. Define `spec fn possibility_set` and `spec fn max_accuracy`; prove with a `proof fn` that any classifier's output belongs to one class in each observation group and therefore cannot exceed the group's maximum fraction. A top-level `fn` can compute the bound for a concrete finite dataset with `ensures` matching the specification. This is a direct contract proof over all classifiers represented by the finite observation partition; it does not require implementing the attack.

Inference: the useful proof pattern is to bound every classifier through the observation partition. It says, “This method returns the best possible accuracy for a given testing set as it makes the lowest possible error for observations mapping to multiple classes.” (Section 6.1, p. 152). It gives the attacker the testing set itself, so the computed maximum is an upper bound for any attack under that dataset. This is still conditional on the trace universe, observation equality, and deterministic D.

## technique: optimal deterministic defense via anonymity sets and supersequences

What it is. A simulatable defense sees only packet lengths, direction, and time, and a deterministic defense maps an input sequence p to one fixed output D(p) (Section 3.4, pp. 145–146). For each p, its anonymity set A(p) contains all inputs with the same output. To make all members indistinguishable while minimizing added packets, D(p) must be the shortest common supersequence (SCS) of A(p). The paper states, “In other words, the optimal solution is to apply the shortest common supersequence function to anonymity sets of input sequences.” (Section 6.2, p. 152).

How to apply it. (1) Choose the client information level: no information, sequence-end information, class-specific information, or full information (Section 6.3, pp. 152–153). (2) Partition traces into anonymity sets consistent with that information. (3) For each set, find a common supersequence that preserves packet order and contains every input as a subsequence (Section 6.2, p. 152). (4) Emit that supersequence, possibly stopping at permitted stopping points when the client knows only sequence end (Section 6.3, p. 153). (5) Define bandwidth overhead as total output length minus input length, divided by total input length (Section 6.2, p. 152). (6) State that exact SCS for multiple sequences is NP-hard, so use an approximation where exact computation is infeasible (Section 6.4, p. 153).

Verus form. Inference: encode a packet sequence as `Seq<int>` and define `spec fn is_subsequence` and `spec fn is_common_supersequence`. A `proof fn` can establish that every input in an anonymity set is a subsequence of the emitted output and that equal outputs imply the stated possibility-set bound. For an exact small set, add an optimality lemma comparing output length with every candidate supersequence. For large sets, verify the approximation's feasibility and measure overhead; Verus cannot prove optimality of a heuristic without a separate approximation theorem. Inference: the privacy proof should be a relation between the concrete Rust padding routine and this sequence specification.

## technique: anonymity-set selection under information limits

The four information levels define how much the defense may condition its output. With no information, all traces share one anonymity set and one supersequence. With sequence-end information, outputs are prefixes of one supersequence. With class information, traces within each page may be clustered into several supersequences and stopping points. Full information permits separate supersequences for every trace but is mainly a theoretical bound (Section 6.3, pp. 152–153).

The clustering procedure is: choose the number of supersequences, randomly choose roots, assign each unclassified sequence to its closest root, then use a two-level process for class-specific information. For class-specific clustering the distance is `2|SCS(p′,q′)| - |p′| - |q′|`, where both prefixes have length `min(|p|,|q|)`; full-information clustering uses the complete traces (Section 6.3, p. 153). Stopping points are the earliest positions where the target maximum uniform accuracy holds (p. 153).

Verus form. Inference: represent the information level and cluster assignment as explicit ghost data. Prove that each runtime output is selected only from its permitted cluster and that stopping prefixes preserve the supersequence relation. Random root choice is not a proof input; record the chosen roots as data and verify the resulting partition. A proof can certify the claimed bound for that partition, while the clustering quality and bandwidth are empirical measures.

## technique: polynomial SCS approximation

What it is. The paper approximates the SCS of n direction sequences with counters c1 through cn. At each step it counts how many current symbols are outgoing; if more than n/4 are outgoing it appends an outgoing packet, otherwise it appends an incoming packet, and increments counters for sequences whose current symbol matches. It stops when every counter reaches sequence length plus one (Section 6.4, p. 153). The n/4 choice reflects the authors' observation that web loads have fewer outgoing packets and reduces overhead. They warn that the algorithm can perform very poorly and claim, citing [13], that polynomial-time SCS approximation cannot have bounded error (p. 153).

How to apply it. (1) Initialize one counter per sequence at 1. (2) Count current outgoing symbols. (3) Append the threshold rule's direction and advance matching counters. (4) Repeat until all counters pass their sequence lengths. (5) Verify every input is a subsequence and measure overhead against the exact definition (Section 6.4, p. 153).

Verus form. Inference: track counters and prove that every consumed input prefix embeds in the output. A termination proof additionally needs every iteration to advance an unfinished sequence. The printed fixed `n/4` threshold does not establish this: if only one outgoing-only sequence remains among four original sequences, the incoming branch advances nothing. Check the implementation or add and verify a progress rule before claiming termination. The paper reports no approximation guarantee for this heuristic; measure overhead and prove subsequence feasibility separately.

## defense evidence, trusted assumptions, and VL threat

On 100 sites with 30 instances each, the authors evaluate sequence-end, class, and full-information defenses against a target uniform accuracy. A class-level defense with maximum uniform accuracy 0.1 has approximately 59% ± 3% overhead and the new attack accuracy 0.068 ± 0.007; the bound says no attack on that dataset can exceed 0.1. For comparison, decoy pages have 130% ± 20% overhead and accuracy 0.30 ± 0.06, while BuFLO has 190% ± 20% and 0.10 ± 0.03 (Section 7, p. 154). The bound is conditional on the dataset used to establish the possibility sets. Section 6.1 explicitly names the training set for a defense's maximum-uniform-accuracy claim and warns that a constructed testing set can contain a page with accuracy 1; Table 2 distinguishes training and testing by information level.

The trusted base includes the trace collection and page-load oracle, the deterministic-defense implementation, equality of packet observations, the chosen testing multiset, and the mathematical implementation of SCS or its approximation. The authors publish attack and defense code, data, feature vectors, clustering, stopping points, and supersequences (Section 8.3, p. 155). They also acknowledge that false alarms overwhelm true positives when sensitive pages occur less than 0.1% of visits (Section 8.2, p. 155).

Inference: the paper supplies a reusable finite privacy proof pattern, but does not evaluate agent-written Rust or Verus. VL can prove an exact finite privacy bound, output-size bound, and subsequence invariant, but it cannot infer that packet traces are complete, that timing and direction are modeled correctly, or that the runtime network preserves the abstraction. The paper already supplies the core universal-bound and supersequence ideas; VL should adapt them to explicit Rust sequence contracts and preserve the denominator, prior, and observation model in every claim.

## techniques for the catalog

- feature-weighted k-NN attack — classify packet traces with a broad weighted feature distance and learn weights that emphasize features a defense leaves open
- possibility-set upper bound — compute the best possible classifier accuracy for each defended observation by its most frequent class
- anonymity-set privacy defense — make every input in an anonymity set map to one deterministic output so the upper bound is explicit
- shortest common supersequence construction — minimize bandwidth among deterministic simulatable defenses by outputting SCS values
- information-level clustering — choose anonymity sets and stopping prefixes according to what the client knows
- counter-based SCS approximation — track consumed prefixes; verify progress before claiming termination and report measured overhead
