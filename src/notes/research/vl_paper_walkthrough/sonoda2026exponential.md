# Exponential Sample Complexity Separation between Flat and Hierarchical Agentic Theorem Provers(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)Tag: `sonoda2026exponential`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10512)## what the paper provesSonoda, Akiyama, and Uezato model verifier-guided theorem proving as offline imitation learning in a deterministic finite-horizon Markov decision process. A flat student predicts tactic actions from fully inlined proof traces. A hierarchical student predicts a reusable proof DAG, high-level graph actions, and local tactics; solved blocks are memoized. Their result is a statistical guarantee: when flattening duplicates a shared subproof exponentially many times, the sufficient sample count certified by their bounds can be exponentially larger for the flat learner. This is a theorem about explicit upper-bound certificates under assumptions, not an empirical benchmark result and not a minimax lower bound.The paper defines statistical provability as average probability of reaching a verified state within a finite budget: `SP_T(π) = E_{s0~q0}[V_T^π(s0)]` (Section 1, p. 2). It separates three error sources: teacher top-k mass omitted by the search shortlist, one-step imitation error, and, for hierarchy, graph coverage error. The abstract summarizes the mechanism: “A hierarchical student instead predicts a reusable proof DAG and solves each shared block once” (Abstract, p. 1).## technique 1: verifier MDP and top-k success boundwhat it is: represent proof search as a deterministic MDP `M_K=(S,A_lo,F_lo,G,ι)`. States are open proof goals and contexts; actions are tactics, inference-rule applications, or lemma calls; the verifier transition either accepts an action or rejects it; solved states are `G`. A policy ranks a finite candidate set at every state, and a fixed search wrapper explores the top-k candidates.when to use it: use this abstraction when measuring a learned prover's finite-budget success rather than only whether a proof exists. It exposes where training error enters the probability of finding a verified proof.how to do it:1. define the formal language, proof states, ordinary tactic alphabet, verifier transition, solved-state set, and initial-state map (Section 2, pp. 2–3)2. condition the teacher distribution on successful traces and define the decision-state mixture3. compute teacher top-k loss `η_k(s)` and average imitation KL error `e(π)` (Section 3, p. 4)4. assume search completeness: if every teacher action appears in the student's top-k lists, the wrapper recovers a proof (Assumption 2, p. 4)5. apply the flat guarantee `E[V_T^π] ≥ 1 − (Lη + c e^p)` from Theorem 1 (Section 4, p. 5)The source states: “A learned prover is a Markov policy” (Section 2, p. 3). This is a modeling choice; it does not claim current LLM agents are literally Markov policies.evidence: Theorem 1 is an analytical bound, with `L` the average successful-trace length, `η` intrinsic shortlist loss, and `e` one-step KL error. Theorem 2 supplies a geometric ERM learning rate under a compact state space, finite action alphabet, probability floor, Lipschitz policies, and a Tsybakov-style margin (Sections 3–4, pp. 4–5).limits and Verus form: the bound assumes deterministic verifier transitions, a complete search wrapper, bounded horizon, and success-conditioned teacher data. It does not measure Lean or Verus tasks.
