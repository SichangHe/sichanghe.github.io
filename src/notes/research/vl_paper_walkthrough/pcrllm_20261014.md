# PCRLLM: Proof-Carrying Reasoning with Large Language Models under Stepwise Logical Constraints
(authored by agents unless marked 🧑)
Tag: `li2025pcrllm`, [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08392)

## purpose and result

PCRLLM makes an LLM emit structured reasoning in a target logic while retaining natural-language task descriptions. The authors claim: “Each output explicitly specifies premises, rules, and conclusions, thereby enabling verification against a target logic.” (Abstract, p. 1). The experiment uses first-order Non-Axiomatic Logic (NAL), two inference steps, JSON output, and graded checking. Inference: for VL, this suggests emitting proof obligations that Verus checks independently of the model that proposed them; the paper does not study Verus.

## technique 1: generate proof-carrying, single-step outputs

What it is: require explicitly structured premises, conclusions, and inference rules. The authors write: “The key idea is to constrain reasoning to single steps so that each step can be verified against a target logic.” (Introduction, p. 2). NAL judgments carry subject, object, copula, frequency, confidence, and an evidential base; results additionally identify the rule (Sec. 4.1, p. 7). The evidential base records premise IDs to prevent counting evidence twice: “Any conclusion derived through inference must carry all premise IDs (to a maximum capacity), and if overlapping IDs are detected, the inference is disallowed.” (Sec. 3.1, p. 5).

When to use it (Inference): when intermediate inferences must be checked or recombined.

How to do it (Sec. 4.1, pp. 6–7):

1. Randomly choose one of nine inference rules, instantiate the two premises with unique terms for S, M, and P, and attach a random evidential base containing at most three IDs. The paper states: “One of the nine inference rules (Fig. 1) is randomly selected to determine first two premises (J1, J2) used, as well as the pending conclusions.” (p. 6).
2. Select a conclusion from step 1 as a premise for step 2, generate the other premise independently, assign the remaining unique term, and choose a possible step-2 conclusion as the goal. Add two distractor premises from randomly selected rules and distinct terms (p. 6).
3. Give the LLM five premises: two from step 1, the independent step-2 premise, and two distractors. Ask for the selected step-2 conclusion with its truth values removed (p. 7).
4. Translate using 20 templates for each copula, five frequency categories, and five expressions per category. The authors specify: “Note that the confidence (c) is omitted here and replaced with the LLM’s answer-level confidence.” (p. 7).
5. Require JSON with top-level `Step 1` and `Step 2`, each containing `Premise 1`, `Premise 2`, and `Results`. Sentences use `s`, `o`, `cp`, `f`, `c`, `eb`, and, for results, `r`. The text specifies a default confidence of 0.9 (p. 7).

Verus form (Inference): represent each step as a Rust data structure and specify the applicable NAL rule and truth functions. A proof can establish that the result follows from its premises under that specification. Chain checking must also establish that step 2 uses a result from step 1. This would establish conformity to the encoded rules; whether extracted premises accurately represent the natural-language problem remains a separate obligation.

Evidence and scope: “We focus on two-step NAL reasoning in this paper, as it represents the simplest form of multi-step reasoning.” (Sec. 4.1, p. 6). The authors fine-tune three clones per backbone on disjoint rule subsets: “All models are fine-tuned on 100 examples” (Sec. 5, p. 10). They use Qwen 2.5/0.5B for 1 epoch, 1.5B for 2 epochs, and 3B for 4 epochs (p. 10). Testing uses “the same test set of 100 examples that uniformly covers all rules” within each experiment (p. 11).

## technique 2: checker-based graded verification

What it is: parse JSON, use the data-construction reasoning engine to generate possible conclusions, and compare the output through bipartite matching. The authors state: “Otherwise, we employ the same reasoning engine used in data construction to generate all possible conclusions and identify the closest match to the LLM’s output through bipartite matching.” (Sec. 4.2, p. 9).

How to do it (Sec. 4.2, pp. 9–10):

1. Give a parsing failure the minimum grade, 0.1. The stated motivation is “to retain gradient utility in reinforcement learning, if applicable” (p. 9); the experiments describe fine-tuning, not reinforcement-learning results.
2. Award each categorical field (`s`, `o`, `cp`, `eb`, `r`) 5 points for an exact match and 0 otherwise. Award each numerical field (`f`, `c`) 25 points for difference ≤ 0.05 and 0 for difference ≥ 0.2. Between those bounds, award `(0.1 + 0.9 × (1 − (diff − 0.05)/0.15))² × 25`. Normalize by the maximum possible score (p. 9).
3. Compare the first-step conclusion with the second-step premises using the same matching method. The paper limits this check to internal structure: “Therefore, we evaluate LLM outputs by analyzing their internal chain structure.” (p. 9).
4. When labels are available, compare the second-step conclusion with the label across all seven fields. Multiply step-wise and inter-step grades for formal conformity; multiply that by the ground-truth grade for testing (pp. 9–10). The authors write: “In testing, the formal conformity grade can further be multiplied with the ground-truth grade, yielding a comprehensive measure of whether the LLM is both logically consistent and successful in completing the reasoning task.” (p. 10).

When to use it (Inference): graded matching can rank partly correct outputs. A threshold on this score permits some discrepancies and does not establish exact logical validity.

Verus form (Inference): specify the score function and prove its bounds. A full score implies categorical equality and numerical agreement within tolerance, rather than exact equality of truth values. Verify exact rule validity separately if VL requires it.

Evidence and limits: “To be comprehensive, we sweep the threshold from 0.1 to 0.9 to show the performance of the LLM network under increasingly strict conditions.” (Sec. 5, p. 11). The denominator is 100 testing cases (p. 11). The authors caution: “Across different model-size experiments (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B), the test sets may differ.” (p. 12). Inference: these cross-size curves do not isolate model size with a fixed test set.

## technique 3: step-wise multi-model recomposition

What it is: rank complete answers by formal conformity (`mb3`), or form nine two-step combinations from three models (`mb9`). The authors describe the latter as: “decompose each model’s answer into steps and recombine them across models to synthesize a new answer (3 first-step possibilities × 3 second-step possibilities).” (Sec. 5, p. 11). Section 4.3 proposes selecting higher-formality answers and recombining individually strong steps (p. 10).

How to do it: obtain each model’s two-step response, score complete answers, and compare combinations of first and second steps using the formal-conformity measure. The experiment also uses DeepSeek R1 to repair broken JSON strings (Sec. 5, p. 10): “We therefore apply DeepSeek R1 [5] as a post-processor to repair broken JSON strings.” Dashed curves show repaired results (Figs. 4–6, pp. 11–13).

Verus form (Inference): verify each proposed step and a composition condition connecting the first conclusion to the second premise. Recombination must preserve evidence constraints as well as sentence compatibility. Score-based filtering could reduce candidate count, but does not prove the remaining candidates valid.

Evidence and limits: for 0.5B, the authors report: “Moreover, mb9 further improves over m3, highlighting the additional benefit of step-wise recomposition.” (Sec. 5, p. 12; subscripts rendered inline). For 3B, they state: “Meanwhile, the performance gap between mb3 and mb9 narrows.” (Sec. 5, p. 13; subscripts rendered inline). They offer stronger individual outputs as a “plausible explanation,” rather than an established cause (p. 13). Overall, “many settings still fail to exceed a valid-answer ratio of 0.2 under relatively loose thresholds” (Sec. 5.1, p. 13). Section 6 acknowledges that scoring “does not fundamentally resolve this complexity bottleneck” as the search space grows with reasoning depth and NAL extensions (p. 14).

## what is proved, trusted, and relevant to VL

Inference: the paper demonstrates graded conformity to an implemented rule engine, rather than a proof of model correctness or of faithful natural-language interpretation. The engine and the interpretation of inputs remain assumptions of that evaluation. The authors explicitly acknowledge that “we cannot attribute which premises originate from which problem description; consequently, hallucination may still occur.” (Sec. 6, pp. 13–14).

Inference: VL could adapt the structured-output and recomposition ideas by emitting proof obligations and requiring Verus to discharge them. Correctly specified rules, evidence handling, and chain compatibility would be necessary; validating extracted premises against the original task would remain separate. The paper supplies no Verus implementation or evaluation.

## techniques for the catalog

- proof-carrying single-step output — require structured premises, conclusions, rule names, truth values, and evidence for step checking

- graded checker matching — enumerate legal conclusions and score categorical equality and numerical agreement

- step-wise recomposition — combine candidate steps from several models and rank resulting chains by formal conformity
