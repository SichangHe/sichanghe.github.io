(authored by agents)

Write the paper as one chain a reader can retell, and
bend every section toward it.
- Abstract and intro: premise; the assumption that is now breaking;
    why existing answers fail; what we do; the findings.
    Cut background that only proves an obvious premise.
- Reduce prior work to a few named failings, e.g., "either opaque or
    unrepresentative", and make each contribution answer one.
- Restate the chain in the conclusion.
- Drop or rewrite a true local finding that points away from the chain.

Trade nuance for a claim that sticks; somewhat imprecise is fine, false is not.
- Replace a careful justification with a blunt claim:
    "focus on high-level information and marginalize the prefix-level
    policies the RPSL fundamentally operates on" → "but lack generality".
- Lead with the plain claim; move counts, populations, and proxies later,
    or define them once instead of repeating them:
    "We find 193 (3.3%) sites with strong evidence of a shared owner" →
    "We find strong evidence that many sites share an owner".
- Keep at most one hedge word ("most", "largely").
- Collapse multi-way breakdowns into the fewest groups that carry the claim;
    merge categories in prose even if the analysis keeps them apart.
- Round numbers outside the results.
    Numbers in the abstract and intro are takeaways, not dataset sizes.
- State an observation plus a modest implication a reviewer cannot argue
    with, not a hypothesis; do not announce novelty readers will assume.

Turn a weakness into a bounded claim, never a headline.
- "Our detector misses the newest LLMs" →
    "these numbers are lower bounds, because ..."; future work goes last.

Write headlines.
- Headings and paragraph openers are claims a skimmer keeps:
    "Most ASes use the RPSL consistently", not "Do ASes use the RPSL consistently?"
- Give the most alarming number its explanation in the same sentence:
    "Although 63% of pairs fail, 99% of those fail because the peering is
    undeclared."
- After an example, add one line on what it proves.

Make the paper flow.
- Section opening: one-sentence approach; a "however" turn naming
    why it is not enough; the challenges as "First, ... Second, ...";
    "Below, we ...".
- Nothing appears that was not promised; answer each promise in order.
- Give each section one question; move material to where the reader asks it
    instead of appending it.
- Cut "interesting" detail and rambling side analyses;
    put the reader's obvious "but" and its one-sentence answer
    where the claim is made.
